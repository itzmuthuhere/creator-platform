The rest earn their price tag mostly in marketing copy — worth knowing which is which before you pay extra.

## Built-in GPS versus connected GPS
A watch with its own GPS chip can track an outdoor run or ride accurately without your phone anywhere nearby. A watch without one relies on your phone's GPS over Bluetooth, so leaving the house without your phone means losing accurate route and pace tracking entirely. This is a real upgrade for runners and cyclists who like to leave the phone at home, and largely irrelevant for anyone who exercises indoors or always carries their phone anyway.

## SpO2 and ECG sensors, useful for trends, not diagnosis
Blood oxygen and heart rhythm sensors on consumer smartwatches are useful for spotting trends over time, an unusual pattern worth mentioning to a doctor, but they aren't certified medical diagnostic devices and shouldn't be treated as one. If you're monitoring a known condition, talk to your doctor about what device, if any, is appropriate. Don't lean on a smartwatch reading as a medical result.

## Display type changes daily visibility more than looks
An AMOLED always-on display stays readable in direct sunlight and shows the time with a glance, at a real cost to battery life. Cheaper LCD or non-always-on AMOLED displays either dim badly in sunlight or need a wrist flick or button press to wake up, which sounds minor until you're checking the time outdoors dozens of times a day. If the watch will be worn outdoors regularly, this is one of the more noticeable upgrades in daily use.

## Battery life is a real trade-off, not just a spec
Simpler fitness-band style wearables with fewer sensors and a basic display commonly run five to seven days on a charge. Full-featured smartwatches with always-on AMOLED, GPS, and constant health tracking often need charging every one to two days. Neither is wrong, but know which experience you're buying before assuming a smartwatch behaves like a fitness band.

## Cellular and eSIM variants are a narrow-use upgrade
A cellular smartwatch lets you take calls, send messages, and stay reachable without your phone nearby, at a real cost premium and often an added monthly charge from your carrier. This is worth paying for only if you regularly leave your phone behind — during workouts, say — and want to stay reachable anyway. For most people who carry their phone everywhere, it's a feature that goes unused.

## Water resistance ratings mean different things
An IP68 rating covers dust and splash resistance but isn't built for swimming. A 5ATM rating is what you want if you plan to swim with the watch, since it's tested for sustained water pressure at depth, not just splashes. Check for the specific swim-rated standard instead of assuming any water resistance number covers swimming.

## What Rotating Bezels and Physical Buttons Actually Add
A physical rotating bezel or crown for navigation, instead of relying entirely on a touchscreen, makes the watch usable with wet or gloved hands and during workouts when touchscreen accuracy drops. Useful for anyone who exercises outdoors in variable weather, though it's largely a preference rather than a necessity for indoor, casual use.

## Band Compatibility Affects Long-Term Cost More Than People Expect
Some smartwatches use a proprietary band attachment that locks you into buying replacement bands only from that brand, often at a premium. Others use a standard lug width compatible with widely available third-party bands. Check band compatibility before buying so you don't end up stuck with limited, expensive replacement options once the included band wears out.

## Why Software Update Support Matters More Than the Spec Sheet
A smartwatch with an impressive feature list but a short history of software updates from its manufacturer can lose functionality or security patches within a couple of years. A brand's track record for how long it typically supports older models is a less visible factor, but it matters just as much for getting real long-term value from a premium purchase.
