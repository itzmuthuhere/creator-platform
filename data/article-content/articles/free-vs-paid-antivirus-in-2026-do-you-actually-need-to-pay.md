Windows and Android both ship with real, active malware protection built in now, running by default with no separate install. That changes the question people usually ask about antivirus in 2026 — it's less about whether you're protected, and more about which extra layer of a paid suite is worth paying a subscription for on top of what's already running.

## What's already covering you by default
Windows comes with Microsoft Defender active out of the box — real-time scanning, cloud-based threat lookups, and ransomware protection, running silently with no separate install. It consistently holds its own against paid competitors in independent testing too. Android runs Google Play Protect in the background, scanning apps at install time and periodically afterward, plus the Play Store's own vetting before an app is even listed. iPhones don't run traditional antivirus at all, because iOS's sandboxing model restricts what any app, malicious or not, can access, which makes a conventional scanner largely redundant there.

## What paid suites add on top
Paid antivirus typically bundles a VPN, a password manager, dark-web or identity monitoring, parental controls, and a firewall dashboard alongside the core scanning engine. The scanning engine itself is rarely dramatically better than Defender's in independent lab tests — the real value proposition is the bundle, not the raw detection rate. If you'd pay for a VPN and a password manager separately anyway, a suite can work out cheaper than buying each piece individually.

## How free antivirus makes its money
Free antivirus products aren't charities — the free tier typically upsells aggressively toward the paid version through frequent pop-ups, and some products have historically drawn criticism for collecting and monetizing browsing data. Read the privacy policy of any third-party free antivirus before installing it, the section on data sharing with partners — that's the actual trade-off, not a missing feature.

## The risk factors that matter more than which antivirus you pick
Most real-world compromises don't come from a virus slipping past a scanner — they come from phishing links, fake job or loan apps sideloaded outside the Play Store, reused passwords after a data breach, and OTP or screen-share scams over phone calls. No antivirus stops you from typing your own password into a fake bank page or approving a scam UPI collect request. Two-factor authentication, a password manager, and avoiding sideloaded APKs collectively do more for your actual security than upgrading your antivirus tier.

## When paying is worth it
Paying makes sense if you want the bundle under one subscription and login, or if you're managing security for a small business where centralized dashboards and support matter. It also makes sense on an older, unpatched Windows version where Defender's protection is less current. For a single, regularly updated personal device, the case for paying purely for "better antivirus" is weak.

## Mobile-Specific Threats a Scanner Never Sees
The bigger mobile risk in 2026 isn't a virus a scanner would catch. It's a fake screen-share or remote-access request during a scam call, where the victim voluntarily grants access instead of malware sneaking in. No antivirus app flags a legitimate-looking screen-share permission you approved yourself. Being suspicious of any unsolicited call asking you to install a remote-access app or share your screen matters more here than any security software running in the background.

## Checking If Defender Is Actually Doing Its Job
Windows Security (search for it in the Start menu) shows Defender's current status, last scan date, and whether real-time protection is active. Worth a quick check if you've never opened it — a misconfigured group policy or a conflicting third-party tool can occasionally leave it silently disabled with no obvious warning on screen.

## Free Third-Party Antivirus, If You Want One Anyway
If Defender and Play Protect still feel insufficient, well-reviewed free options exist without the aggressive upsell pattern common to older free antivirus products. Check recent independent lab results (AV-Test, AV-Comparatives) instead of app store ratings alone — ratings are easy to manipulate, and lab results measure detection rate under controlled conditions.

## iOS Isn't Immune, Just Differently Protected
iPhone users sometimes take the lack of antivirus apps to mean iOS can't be compromised at all. That's not quite right — it means the attack surface shifts toward phishing links, malicious configuration profiles, and jailbroken devices instead of traditional file-based malware. The practical takeaway is the same regardless of platform: most real compromises start with a person clicking or approving something they shouldn't, not a scanner failing to catch a file.

## How Often to Actually Check In on This
Security settings aren't a set-and-forget item. A quarterly five-minute check — confirm Defender or Play Protect is active, review which apps have accessibility or admin permissions, update the OS if it's behind — catches most drift before it becomes a real gap, without needing to think about security constantly in between.

## A sane default setup for most people
Keep Windows Defender and automatic updates on, keep Play Protect enabled, use a password manager, turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it's offered, and avoid installing APKs from outside the Play Store. That combination covers the realistic risks most people face day to day — a paid suite is worth adding on top of it only if you want the bundled VPN, password manager, or monitoring tools that come with it.
