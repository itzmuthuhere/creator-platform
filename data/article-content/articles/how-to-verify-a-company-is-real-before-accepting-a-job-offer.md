Verifying a company before you accept its offer takes about fifteen minutes and doesn't require any special access — CIN, GST registration, and domain history are all public record. Here's the sequence worth running through before you hand in your resignation letter.

## Check the company's registration on the MCA portal
Go to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs website (mca.gov.in) and use the Check Company/LLP Master Data search under the MCA Services section. Search by the company name — you should find its CIN (Corporate Identification Number), date of incorporation, registered office address, and current status (active, dormant, or struck off). A company claiming to be a large, established firm but showing as recently incorporated, or worse, struck off, is a serious red flag. Note that small startups can be legitimate and recently incorporated too, so treat this as one data point, not a pass-fail test on its own.

## Cross-check the GST registration
If the company mentions a GSTIN on its offer letter or website, verify it on the GST portal (services.gst.in) under Search Taxpayer. This confirms the GSTIN is real, active, and registered to the entity name you were told, not a mismatched or cancelled registration.

## Verify the domain and communication channel
Legitimate companies communicate from a corporate email domain matching their actual website, not from a generic Gmail or Outlook address. Check that the domain in the offer letter's sender address matches the company's official website domain exactly — scammers often register a lookalike domain that's off by one character. Also check how long the domain has existed using any public WHOIS lookup tool; a domain registered a few weeks ago claiming to belong to a company that's supposedly been operating for years is inconsistent.

## Look for independent, non-company-controlled evidence
- LinkedIn: does the company have an established page with employees who list it as their current employer, not just a handful of accounts created recently?
- Review platforms like Glassdoor or AmbitionBox: is there a history of reviews over time, not a sudden cluster all posted in the same week?
- Google the exact office address given — does it map to a real, occupied commercial space, or a residential flat, or nothing at all?

## Red flags that matter more than any single document check
Any of these should make you stop and dig deeper regardless of what the paperwork shows: being asked to pay a security deposit, training fee, or equipment fee before joining; refusal to do a live video interview; job offers that arrive without any interview process at all; unusually high pay for minimal stated requirements; and pressure to respond or pay within hours.

## Checking Employee Reviews for Authenticity, Not Just Star Rating
A high average rating built from reviews all posted within the same short window is a weaker signal than a lower rating built from reviews spread naturally across years, since a sudden cluster of five-star reviews is a known pattern used to inflate a company's profile artificially. Reading a handful of the actual written reviews, not just the star average, and checking whether specific, plausible details appear — team names, project types, office locations — gives a better sense of authenticity than the number alone.

## What a Legitimate Offer Letter Should Contain
A genuine offer letter specifies designation, compensation structure, reporting manager or team, and a joining date, on company letterhead with a verifiable signatory. Vague offer letters missing these specifics, or ones that arrive as a plain email with no letterhead at all, warrant more scrutiny before you act on them, especially before resigning from a current role based on it.

## Why Verifying Matters More During a Hot Job Market
Fake job offers tend to spike whenever hiring activity in a sector is picking up, since scammers deliberately mimic the pattern of real hiring surges to blend in and catch people who are actively job-hunting and eager to move quickly. Running the same fifteen-minute verification checklist regardless of how legitimate an offer feels in the moment costs little, and protects against exactly the situations where urgency and excitement make people skip due diligence they'd normally do.

## What to Do If You've Already Resigned Based on a Suspicious Offer
If verification raises real doubts after you've already given notice, contact your current employer's HR immediately to discuss withdrawing or pausing your resignation. Most companies would rather retain you a little longer than have you walk into a fraudulent situation, and being upfront about the reason tends to get a more understanding response than trying to reverse course without explanation.

## Checking Whether the Company Actually Remits Provident Fund
For companies claiming a meaningful headcount, checking whether they're registered with EPFO and actively remitting provident fund contributions is a strong, hard-to-fake signal, since it requires ongoing compliance with a government system rather than a one-time registration. The EPFO member portal lets you check an establishment's registration status by name, and an established company with dozens or hundreds of claimed employees showing no EPFO registration at all is worth questioning directly.

## Reaching Out to Current or Former Employees Directly
Beyond reading reviews, sending a short, polite message to a current or former employee found through LinkedIn, asking one or two specific questions about their actual experience, often gets an honest answer that a public review platform won't fully capture. Most people are willing to answer a brief, respectful question from someone considering the same company, and a pattern of no one responding or several people declining to discuss the company at all is itself informative.

## Distinguishing a Direct Employer From a Recruitment Agency Impersonating One
Some scams work by impersonating a legitimate recruitment agency rather than the hiring company itself, using a real agency's name and branding without the agency's knowledge. If you're contacted by a staffing or recruitment firm on behalf of a company, verify independently through the agency's own official website and contact number, not any contact details supplied in the outreach message itself, since a fake outreach can convincingly clone a real agency's identity.

## Checking the Interviewer's Own Professional History
A LinkedIn profile for the person claiming to interview you should show a reasonably established history at the company, not an account created shortly before contacting you with no prior professional activity. Genuine employees at real companies typically have LinkedIn histories spanning multiple roles and years, connections from actual colleagues, and content or engagement consistent with someone who has worked in that industry for a while.

## What a Genuine Video Interview Confirms
Beyond just agreeing to a video call, pay attention to whether the interview happens through the company's own scheduling and video tools, rather than an ad hoc personal video link sent over WhatsApp, and whether the interviewer can speak knowledgeably about the specific role, team, and company details beyond a generic script. A rushed, scripted-feeling conversation with someone unable to answer specific follow-up questions about the actual work is a signal worth weighing alongside every other check.

## What a Background Verification Request Tells You
Somewhat counterintuitively, a company that runs a formal background verification on you, checking previous employment, education, and sometimes a police verification, is usually a positive signal rather than a red flag, since this level of process costs the company time and money and isn't something a scam operation bothers investing in. A job offer with zero verification of any kind, moving straight from a first conversation to a signed offer, is the pattern worth being more cautious about, not less.

## Understanding Why This Matters More for Remote and Work-From-Home Roles
Fully remote roles, especially ones sourced entirely through a job portal or social media with no in-person component at any stage, carry higher fraud risk than roles involving at least one physical office visit or a formally scheduled in-person round, simply because every trust-building signal a real office visit would normally provide is absent. This doesn't mean remote roles are inherently suspicious, most aren't, but it does mean the verification steps above deserve more weight, not less, when nothing about the process happens in person.

## Keep the Paper Trail Even After You've Decided to Join
Save the original offer email, the MCA search result, and any correspondence in one folder, since this record is useful again if any dispute arises during your notice period or early days at the new role, and it costs nothing to keep beyond a few minutes of filing, well spent given what it protects against if something later turns out to be off.

## What to Do If the Company Pushes Back on Verification
A legitimate employer has no reason to be defensive when you mention you're doing standard due diligence before resigning from a current role, and most HR teams treat it as completely normal. Genuine annoyance, stalling, or pressure to skip straight to signing without answering a direct question about the company's registration or the interviewer's background is itself worth treating as a signal, since it runs against how confident, established employers typically respond to a candidate simply being careful before a major decision.

## The one call that settles it
If doubt remains after all this, call the company's official number — found independently through its website or a search engine, never the number listed in the offer email — and ask HR to confirm the offer and the recruiter's identity directly. Genuine employers handle this request without hesitation, which alone tells you most of what you need to know.
