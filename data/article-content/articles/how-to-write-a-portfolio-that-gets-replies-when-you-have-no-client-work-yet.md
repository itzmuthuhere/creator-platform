Nobody wants to hire you without proof of work, and you can't easily get proof of work without someone hiring you first — that's the trap most people run into when they're starting out. The usual response is to build three or four generic practice projects, a to-do app, a food-delivery clone, a redesign of some random app nobody asked for, and drop them into a gallery. Hiring managers scroll past this constantly, because they've seen the same handful of practice projects hundreds of times and have no way to tell whether you understood the problem or just followed a tutorial.

## Why "practice work" gets scrolled past
A gallery of screenshots with no context asks the viewer to guess what problem you solved and why your solution was the right one. Nobody does that guessing for free — a recruiter or founder looking at portfolios spends seconds per project, not minutes. If the work doesn't visibly answer what was broken, what you did, and what changed, it doesn't matter how polished the visuals are.

## Tear down a real, existing product instead
Pick a company you'd like to work with and do the work you'd do for them, unpaid, on a small visible slice of their product. If you're a designer, redesign one screen of their checkout flow and explain what specific friction you removed. If you're a copywriter, rewrite their homepage headline and the lines under it, and explain the reasoning. If you're a developer, find a slow or broken part of a real site and fix it in a sandbox, then write up what you found. This is harder than doing a generic project, but it's the fastest way to get a reply — because you've done work about them, specifically, not work about yourself.

## Frame each project as a case study, not a gallery image
Every entry should follow roughly the same shape: the problem in one sentence, the constraint that made it hard, what you did, and what changed — even if "what changed" is a reasoned prediction rather than a measured result, since you didn't have real users. "This reduced the number of form fields from 9 to 4, which should cut abandonment on mobile" is a case study. A screenshot with no caption is not.

## Record a short walkthrough instead of writing five paragraphs
A two-to-three-minute screen recording where you talk through your reasoning does more work than a wall of text, because it shows you thinking, not just presenting a finished artifact. It also filters for confidence — anyone can polish a static image, fewer people can explain their decisions out loud without hedging. Attach the recording next to each project instead of replacing the write-up with it; some readers skim text, others will watch.

## Keep the portfolio to one page
Three strong, well-explained pieces of work beat ten thin ones. A single scrollable page — problem statements up top, work below, contact info at the bottom — gets read in full far more often than a multi-page site with a nav bar, because every extra click loses a chunk of your audience. Save the sprawling version for later, once you have real client work to fill it with.

## What to Do If a Teardown Feels Presumptuous
Some people hesitate to redesign or critique someone else's real product without permission, worried it'll come across as arrogant rather than helpful. Framing matters here: present it explicitly as a practice exercise you did because you admire the company or product, not as an unsolicited claim that their team got it wrong. "I used your checkout flow as a practice project because I think your product is well built, here's what I explored" reads completely differently from "here's what's wrong with your site."

## How Many Projects Is Actually Enough to Start Applying
Two strong, well-documented pieces of work are enough to start reaching out and applying — you don't need to wait for five or six before you're "ready." A portfolio with two thoughtful case studies gets more attention than one with six thin ones, and waiting for more before applying just delays the real feedback that would improve your approach.

## What to Do With the Feedback You Get
Most cold outreach, even good outreach, gets more silence than replies, that's normal, not a sign the approach is wrong. When you do get a reply, even a rejection, ask directly what would have made the work stronger or more relevant. This kind of direct feedback from someone in your target industry is worth more than another week spent polishing a project in isolation.

## Updating the Portfolio as Real Work Comes In
Once you land actual paid client work, even one small project, replace your weakest self-initiated piece with it. Real work with a real client's context, even a small one, generally outweighs a self-initiated project in credibility. Keep the strongest self-initiated pieces until you have enough real client work to fill the gap entirely — there's no need to remove them just because they weren't paid.

## The Underlying Point
None of this is about tricking anyone into thinking you have more experience than you do. It's about giving a hiring manager something real to evaluate instead of asking them to imagine your ability from a generic project they've seen a hundred times before.

## Why a Teardown Beats a Redesign From Scratch
Starting completely from scratch on a brand new concept lets you avoid engaging with real constraints, an existing brand, existing users, existing technical limitations, all of which a genuine client project would impose. A teardown of something real forces you to work within those same constraints, which is precisely the skill a hiring manager is trying to evaluate, not your ability to imagine an unconstrained ideal that no real client would ever offer you.

## What to Do If You Genuinely Have Zero Portfolio Pieces Yet
Starting from complete zero, pick one small, well-scoped teardown this week rather than planning an ambitious multi-project portfolio before doing anything, since a single strong, well-documented piece of work you can point to today beats an elaborate plan for five pieces you haven't started. Momentum from finishing one small thing tends to carry into the next far more reliably than a big unfinished plan does, and the first finished piece is always the hardest one to produce.

## Choosing a Company You'd Actually Want to Work For
Picking a teardown target purely because it's a recognizable big name isn't as effective as picking one you'd be excited to work with, since genuine enthusiasm shows through in the writeup's depth and specificity far more convincingly than a generic exercise on a company chosen just for its brand recognition, and that authenticity is exactly what a good hiring manager is trying to sense when reading your work.

## Treating Rejection as Data, Not a Verdict
A company that doesn't reply, or replies with a pass, isn't necessarily saying the work was weak, timing, budget, and internal hiring freezes affect outcomes just as much as quality does, so resist reading too much into silence and keep sending the same strong work to the next few targets instead of second-guessing the work itself after one quiet week.

## Why This Approach Also Builds Real Skill, Not Just a Portfolio
Sitting down to analyze a real product's problems and articulate a solution is harder and more instructive than following a tutorial's prescribed steps, so the exercise itself makes you measurably better at the job, independent of whether it ever gets you a reply from the specific company you chose to study.

## Why Showing Your Process Matters as Much as the Final Result
A polished final screenshot alone tells a hiring manager you can produce something clean, but it says nothing about how you got there. Including a rough early draft alongside the finished version, even briefly, demonstrates the actual thinking that separates someone who can iterate from someone who got lucky on the first attempt.

## What to Do If You're Torn Between Two Very Different Teardown Targets
Pick the one that lets you say more, not the one with the more impressive name attached. A smaller, less famous product where you can identify three genuine, specific problems beats a huge brand where you can only manage one generic observation everyone else has already made too.

## Send it personalized, not as a mass link
The portfolio link itself rarely gets you a reply — the message around it does. When you send it, reference the specific project inside it that's relevant to the person you're messaging, rather than a generic "check out my portfolio." That one line tells the reader you didn't send the same message to fifty people, which is exactly what makes them open the link at all.
Three well-argued pieces of real, unpaid work take longer to put together than ten toy projects, but they're the ones that get read — because each one answers the question a hiring manager is silently asking: can this person think through a real problem the way my team needs them to.
