Most LinkedIn advice is written for a human reading your profile top to bottom, and that's the wrong target if your actual goal is getting found by a recruiter in the first place. Recruiters mostly don't scroll — they run a search with specific terms, get back a list of matching profiles, and skim headlines from there. What gets you into that results list, and what makes a good impression once someone opens your profile, are two different problems, and most advice only addresses the second one.

## How recruiter search works
LinkedIn Recruiter, the paid tool most corporate recruiters use, lets them search by exact keywords, current title, past titles, skills, location, and school, combined with AND/OR logic, much like a Google search with filters. A recruiter searching for "React Native" AND "Bangalore" AND "current" will only surface you if those exact terms exist somewhere structured on your profile — not if you wrote "mobile app development" instead. This is why keyword precision matters more than clever phrasing for getting found in the first place.

## Your headline is the highest-leverage line you'll write
The default headline is your current job title, which is often the least useful string for search purposes — "Associate II" tells a recruiter nothing. Rewrite it to include your actual role, your core skill area, and optionally your specialization — something like "Backend Developer | Java, Spring Boot | Building scalable APIs" does far more search and scanning work than a generic title. This one line shows up in search results, in comments, and in connection requests, so it's read far more often than any other part of your profile.

## The skills section is a search filter, not decoration
Recruiters frequently filter by the specific skills listed on your profile, and LinkedIn's own skill-matching weights the skills with the most endorsements more heavily. List the 10-15 skills most relevant to the roles you want, not just the trendy ones, and ask a few colleagues to endorse the three or four that matter most. Remove skills you no longer use; a long, unfocused list dilutes relevance for the ones that matter.

## Write the About section for a scanner, not a reader
Assume the reader gives it eight seconds. Front-load your current role, your core skill stack, and one concrete outcome in the first two lines — that's the part visible before "see more" gets clicked, and most people never click it. Save the narrative and personal interests for after the fold. Repeat your most important keywords naturally here too, since About-section text is also searchable.

## Activity matters more than most people think
LinkedIn's algorithm and many recruiters both weight recent activity — profiles that show up as active through a post, a comment, or an article share rank higher in relevance and simply look more "real" to a recruiter deciding whether to spend an InMail credit on you. You don't need to post daily; commenting thoughtfully on posts in your field a few times a week keeps your profile active without requiring you to become a content creator.

## The Featured Section Most Profiles Leave Empty
The Featured section sits prominently near the top of a profile and lets you pin a project, article, or presentation directly where a recruiter's eye lands early, yet most profiles leave it blank entirely. Pinning one strong, specific piece of work here — a case study, a GitHub repo, a published article — gives a recruiter something concrete to evaluate in the few seconds they spend on your profile before moving to the next search result.

## Recommendations Carry More Weight Than Most People Assume
A handful of specific, detailed recommendations from former managers or colleagues function as third-party verification in a way self-written profile text can't, and recruiters do read them, particularly for senior roles where trust matters more than keyword matching. Asking a former manager directly, with a short reminder of a specific project you worked on together, tends to produce a far more useful recommendation than a generic "would you write me a recommendation" request.

## Why a Photo and Banner Still Matter for Search Results
Profiles without a photo get filtered out or deprioritized in some recruiter searches entirely, since a blank avatar reads as an inactive or fake account. A custom banner image, even a simple one stating your role and specialty, adds another layer of context a recruiter absorbs before reading a single word of your About section.

## Why a Second Set of Eyes Catches What You Can't
Asking a friend in a similar field to search for you using terms a recruiter would plausibly use, then reporting back whether your profile surfaces, is a more honest test of real-world findability than anything you can judge just by reading your own profile.

## Why Your Job Title Field Deserves as Much Care as Your Headline
The actual title field under each work experience entry is separately searchable from your headline, and a recruiter search combining a specific title with a specific company type or industry will miss your profile if your listed title is an internal-only designation nobody outside your company would search for. Adding the more commonly recognized industry-standard title alongside your actual internal designation, in parentheses if needed, closes this specific and easily overlooked gap that costs profiles real visibility without the person ever realizing why.

## Turning Off the Wrong Privacy Setting Without Realizing It
A profile set to a more private visibility level, sometimes changed unintentionally while adjusting an unrelated setting, can exclude you from public search results and limit what a recruiter sees even before they open your profile at all. Periodically checking your profile's visibility settings under Settings, then Visibility, confirms your public profile is showing what you think it's showing to someone who isn't already a connection, rather than assuming a setting changed months ago is still exactly what you intended.

## Why Consistency Across Job Portals Matters Beyond LinkedIn Itself
Recruiters frequently cross-reference a LinkedIn profile against a Naukri or Indeed listing for the same candidate, and a noticeable mismatch in dates, titles, or claimed responsibilities between platforms raises a credibility question that a perfectly optimized LinkedIn profile alone can't offset. Keeping the core facts, dates, titles, company names, identical across every platform you're active on is a small discipline that protects the trust a strong profile otherwise builds.

## Revisiting the Whole Profile Every Few Months
Skills, titles, and priorities shift faster than most people update their LinkedIn profile to match, so setting a recurring reminder every few months to re-read the headline and About section with fresh eyes keeps the profile aligned with where your career is now, not where it was a year or two ago.

## What Happens When a Profile Goes Stale for Too Long
A profile with no updates or activity for over a year tends to rank lower in relevance even with strong keyword coverage, since LinkedIn's own ranking system factors in recency alongside relevance, meaning even a perfectly optimized profile benefits from occasional small updates that signal it's actively maintained rather than abandoned.

## What to Put in the Custom URL Instead of the Default String
LinkedIn assigns a random string of numbers to your profile URL by default, and editing it to a clean version of your name under Settings, Edit Public Profile URL makes the link far more shareable on a resume or business card, and looks more deliberate and professional to anyone who clicks through from a search result or a shared link.

## Adding Certifications in the Right Place, Not Just the Summary
LinkedIn has a dedicated Licenses & Certifications section separate from the About summary, and certifications placed there show up as their own searchable entries with issue dates, which recruiters filtering by specific credentials will find. Burying a certification inside a paragraph of text means it never surfaces in that kind of targeted recruiter search at all, no matter how relevant or hard-earned it is.

## Why Endorsements From Strangers Can Actually Hurt Credibility
A skill endorsed by dozens of people you've never worked with, common on profiles that joined endorsement-swap groups, reads as inflated rather than impressive to anyone checking closely. A handful of endorsements from people who can plausibly vouch for that specific skill carries more real weight than a large number from unfamiliar names.

## India-specific details that change visibility
A few India-specific details matter too: set your location to the actual city you're targeting rather than just "India," since recruiters filtering by city won't find a country-level profile, and state explicitly if you're open to relocating, because recruiters rarely assume it. Many recruiters also search by college and past employer names for alumni networks, so use the institution's full, standard name in your education and work history rather than an abbreviation only you'd recognize. Turning on "Open to Work" with recruiter-only visibility is worth doing too — it adds you to a dedicated search pool while staying invisible to your current employer's own recruiters. A profile that's easy to find is worth more than one that just looks polished.
