The verified purchase badge used to be a decent filter against fake reviews. It no longer is, because paid reviewers now routinely buy the product for real, post the review, and get refunded separately, earning the badge without it costing them anything. The tells that still work are less obvious than checking for that badge, but they take just as little time to run through.

## Check the reviewer's history, not just the review
Click through to a reviewer's profile where the platform allows it. A reviewer with only a handful of reviews, all five stars, posted across completely unrelated product categories in a short window, is a pattern strongly associated with paid or incentivized reviewing. A reviewer with a longer, more varied history including some critical or mixed reviews is a more credible signal.

## Look at the timing pattern of reviews, not just the count
Open the review section sorted by date rather than by relevance. A sudden cluster of five-star reviews posted within a few days of each other, especially soon after a listing goes live, is a common pattern for a coordinated review campaign. A rating built up gradually and steadily over months looks organic in a way that a sudden spike does not.

## Read the three-star reviews specifically
Extreme ratings, both five-star and one-star, are the ones most likely to be manipulated, either paid positive reviews or, less commonly, targeted negative ones from competitors or bad-faith actors. Reviews in the three-star range tend to be the most detailed and balanced, written by people who used the product and have specific, mixed feedback rather than a blanket reaction either way.

## Cross-check the rating across platforms
If the same product is listed on more than one platform, compare the overall rating and the general tone of feedback across them. A product rated highly on one platform and noticeably lower on another, for the identical model, is worth a closer look, since it suggests the rating on at least one platform does not reflect the product's actual real-world performance.

## Watch for repeated, specific complaints
A handful of scattered one-star reviews about unrelated issues, delivery problems, packaging, and so on, are normal noise on any large platform. What is worth taking seriously is several independent reviewers describing the exact same specific defect, a component failing at the same point, a feature not working as advertised in the same way. That kind of repeated specificity across unconnected reviewers is a strong signal of a genuine, consistent product issue.

## Check the questions and answers section
The community Q&A section on most listings tends to attract more honest, specific answers from actual buyers than the review section does, partly because it gets less attention from review manipulation efforts. A direct question about battery life after six months, or how a product performs in a specific use case, often gets a more useful answer here than from scanning star ratings alone.

## What a Sudden Rating Drop After a Product Update Means
Watch for a product whose rating was strong for months, then dropped noticeably after a specific date. This often signals a manufacturing change, a cost-cutting revision, or a batch quality issue that the listing itself won't announce. Sorting reviews by date and looking at the last month or two, rather than the all-time average, catches this pattern that an aggregate star rating hides completely.

## Photos and Videos in Reviews Are Worth More Than Text
Genuine buyer photos, especially ones showing the product in actual use, packaging, or a specific defect, are far harder to fake at scale than written text, which is why they're one of the more reliable signals available. A listing with dozens of five-star text reviews and zero buyer photos, on a product type people would normally photograph, is itself worth treating with more scepticism.

## Why Extremely Fast Review Accumulation Is a Red Flag
A new listing that accumulates hundreds of reviews within its first week, before the product has plausibly been used long enough for genuine feedback to form, is a strong sign of purchased or incentivized reviews rather than organic buyer feedback building up naturally. This matters even more for a product type meant for longer-term use — a mattress, an appliance, a piece of furniture — where a real opinion simply can't form that fast.

## Reverse-Searching Product Images Sometimes Reveals More Than Reviews Do
For unfamiliar brands specifically, a reverse image search on the main product photo occasionally reveals the same generic product being sold under several different brand names at different price points, a sign of a rebranded, unbranded factory product rather than anything the seller developed. This doesn't necessarily mean the product is bad, but the review count and content matter even more as a result.

## A Habit Worth Building
None of these checks take more than a minute or two individually. Running two or three of them together before a purchase over a few thousand rupees is a worthwhile habit — cheaper by far than the return-and-refund process for a product that turns out not to match what its reviews implied.

## Why Reviews for Complex Electronics Deserve Extra Scrutiny
Products with genuine technical complexity, appliances with several moving parts, electronics with software components, tend to attract more manipulated reviews precisely because most buyers can't independently verify technical claims themselves, making a glowing but vague review harder to distinguish from a genuine one without the specific checks covered above. Weighing a detailed, technically specific review far more heavily than a short enthusiastic one matters more for this category than for something simple and easy to judge firsthand, where even a fake review can't say much beyond a generic compliment.

## What a Seller's Response to Negative Reviews Reveals
Beyond the reviews themselves, checking whether a seller responds constructively to genuine complaints, offering a fix or replacement, versus responding defensively or not at all, gives a useful read on how a return or warranty issue is likely to be handled if something goes wrong with your own purchase, information the star rating alone says nothing about and that most buyers never think to check before purchasing.

## Why a Slightly Lower Rating With More Reviews Can Be More Trustworthy
A product sitting at 4.3 stars across ten thousand reviews has statistically absorbed far more genuine, varied buyer experience than one sitting at a suspiciously perfect 4.9 across only two hundred, and treating the second as automatically better purely because the number is higher ignores how much easier a small review pool is to manipulate convincingly compared to a large one.

## Why a Little Skepticism Saves More Than It Costs
None of these checks require distrust of every listing you encounter, just a proportional amount of scrutiny for anything above a casual, low-stakes purchase, since the two or three minutes spent verifying cost far less than dealing with a return process for a product that never matched what its reviews promised.

## What to Do When You Genuinely Can't Tell Either Way
For a product where every check above comes back ambiguous, neither clearly manipulated nor clearly trustworthy, defaulting to a smaller initial purchase where possible, or checking whether the seller offers a genuine return window, hedges the uncertainty better than either fully trusting or fully dismissing a rating you can't confidently read either way.

## Building This Into a Habit Rather Than a One-Off Effort
The first few times running through these checks will feel slow and deliberate, but like any habit it compresses with repetition, until spotting the telltale signs of a manipulated listing becomes almost automatic within a few purchases, no longer requiring the conscious checklist at all.

## Why Regional Language Reviews Sometimes Get Overlooked
On platforms with a large Indian user base, reviews written in Hindi or another regional language often carry useful, specific feedback that a quick English-only skim misses entirely. Toggling the review language filter, where available, or simply scrolling past reviews that default to being hidden behind a "translate" prompt, surfaces feedback a lot of shoppers never see.

## What a Verified Purchase Badge Still Tells You, Even If Imperfect
Despite the workaround covered earlier, a verified badge still filters out a meaningful share of reviews from people who never bought the product at all, phantom reviews posted without any purchase whatsoever. It's a weaker signal than it used to be, not a useless one, and still worth checking alongside the other signals rather than discarding entirely.

## A quick verification checklist
- Sort reviews by date and check for suspicious clustering
- Spot-check a few reviewer profiles for review history and variety
- Read the three-star reviews before the five-star ones
- Compare the rating for the identical model across at least two platforms
- Look for the same specific complaint repeated by unrelated reviewers
- Check the Q&A section for direct, specific buyer answers

None of this guarantees a perfect read on every single listing, but run together, these checks give a far more reliable picture than the star rating alone ever will, since the star rating is exactly the number manipulation is built to move.
