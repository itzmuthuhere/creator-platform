A product listed at "62% off" and the same product listed at "15% off" can be sitting at the exact same street price on the exact same day. The percentage is doing the selling, not the price. Indian sale seasons produce walls of red discount tags like this constantly, and separating the real markdowns from the inflated ones takes about two minutes once you know what to check.

## Check the price history, not the price today
Browser extensions and price-tracking tools that log a product's price over weeks or months exist for this, and several of them work on Amazon India and Flipkart listings. If a product's so-called sale price today is roughly the same as, or even higher than, what it sold for a month ago, the discount tag is decoration, not a real deal. This single check catches most inflated discounts instantly.

## Compare the same product across platforms at the same time
Open the identical model on two or three sites side by side before buying. Retailers time their biggest markdowns around the same festive windows, so if one platform's sale price is higher than another's regular price elsewhere, the discount is measured against an artificial base, not the actual market rate for that product.

## Read what the discount is calculated from
Many listings show a discount off MRP, the manufacturer's printed maximum retail price, rather than off the platform's own recent selling price. MRP is frequently set high on purpose and rarely reflects what the product sells for even outside sale periods. A large percentage off MRP can still mean you are paying close to the everyday price.

## Watch for the coupon-and-bank-offer stack in the headline number
The big price shown during a sale often already includes an instant discount that only applies with a specific bank's credit card, plus a coupon you have to manually apply, plus sometimes an exchange bonus. If you do not have that exact card or that old device to exchange, your real price is higher than the headline number. Read the full breakdown before comparing it to any other listing.

## Fake urgency is a pricing tell, not just a UX pattern
Countdown timers that reset after refreshing, or 'only 2 left' labels that persist across days, are common enough that they should not influence your decision at all. Genuine limited stock does not usually resolve itself back to the same number every time you revisit the page.

## Why Some Genuine Discounts Still Look Suspicious
Not every large percentage-off tag is fake. Genuine clearance of old inventory, discontinued models, or a brand's own liquidation event can produce legitimately steep discounts. The difference from an inflated fake discount is that a genuine clearance sale is usually explicitly labeled as such — discontinued stock, last few units — rather than presented as a routine limited-time flash sale that recurs every few weeks on the same product.

## Checking Seller Reputation Alongside the Price
An unusually steep discount from a third-party marketplace seller with few reviews or a recently created storefront carries more risk than the same discount from the platform's own official store or a well-established seller. Checking seller rating and review count takes a few seconds and catches a different category of risk than price-history checking alone — counterfeit or misrepresented goods, rather than simply an inflated discount tag.

## What to Do When Price History Tools Show No Data
Newer or less popular listings sometimes have no price history available at all. In that case, comparing the listed price directly against the same product on a second platform, or against a manufacturer's own official store price, is the fallback check. Absence of price history data is not itself a red flag, but it does mean skipping straight to a manual cross-platform comparison rather than relying on an automated check alone.

## Why This Habit Pays Off Beyond Any Single Purchase
Building the reflex to check price history and seller reputation before any purchase, not just during a big sale event, changes how you shop generally. Inflated discount tags show up outside festive sale windows too, on everyday listings that simply borrow the same visual language of urgency to look like a limited-time deal when they're not.

## Why Bundled Deals Deserve Their Own Separate Math
A phone bundled with a case, earbuds, or a gift voucher can look like a bigger discount than it is, since the bundled items are often priced generously to inflate the perceived value of the whole package. Pricing out the main product alone against its standalone listing price elsewhere, and treating the bundled extras as a nice-to-have rather than counting their inflated stated value toward your savings, gives a far more honest picture of what the deal is worth. A ₹500 "free" accessory that would sell standalone for ₹150 is not really ₹500 of savings, no matter what the bundle page claims.

## Understanding "Flash Sale" Pricing Psychology
A flash sale's entire design goal is to compress decision time so you buy before comparing anything, which is exactly why the checks above matter more during a flash sale than a regular listing. The urgency is the product being sold, not the discount. Slowing down when a page is actively pressuring you to hurry is a useful reflex, since genuine deals don't usually depend on you deciding within minutes, and a product still worth buying tomorrow at a similar price was never really a once-in-a-lifetime window to begin with.

## How Retailers Set an Artificially High "Before" Price
Some sellers briefly list a product at an inflated price for a short window right before a sale starts, purely so the sale price can be measured against that inflated number rather than the item's real recent selling price, a practice sometimes called price anchoring. A price-history tool catches this specific tactic instantly, since the brief price spike shows up clearly on the chart even if you never saw the inflated listing yourself, which is exactly why relying on the platform's own claimed discount percentage alone is never enough on its own.

## Checking Whether a Coupon Code Actually Applies to What's in Your Cart
Coupon codes advertised prominently on a listing page sometimes have category exclusions, minimum cart values, or apply only to specific variants, none of which are obvious until checkout. Applying the code and checking the actual total before finalizing a purchase, rather than assuming the advertised discount applies automatically to your specific cart, avoids the common disappointment of a coupon failing at the last step, sometimes without any clear error message explaining why.

## Why Comparing Against Your Own Purchase History Helps Too
If you've bought the same or a similar product from the same platform before, checking your own past order history for what you paid gives a personal price anchor that's harder to manipulate than a listing's own claimed "before" price. This is especially useful for recurring purchases like electronics accessories, printer cartridges, or household staples you buy periodically, where your own history is a more honest baseline than any marketing claim on the page.

## Why Sale-Day Stock Numbers Reset at Midnight
Some platforms deliberately reset a "limited stock" counter at the start of each sale day, even for products that were never running low, purely to recreate urgency for shoppers checking back the next morning. A counter that shows the exact same number two mornings in a row is a strong sign the scarcity was never real to begin with.

## Why International Sellers on Indian Platforms Need a Separate Check
A product shipped from overseas through a marketplace often carries import duties or delayed delivery windows not clearly stated in the headline price, meaning the actual landed cost can run higher than a domestic seller's slightly higher-looking listing. Check the full checkout total, not just the item price, before assuming the cheaper-looking option is cheaper.

## What a Price-Match Guarantee Actually Covers
Some retailers offer to match a lower price found elsewhere, but the fine print often excludes flash sales, coupon-adjusted prices, or marketplace sellers, meaning the guarantee applies to a narrower set of situations than the marketing suggests. Read the specific exclusions listed in the terms before assuming a price match will cover the exact comparison you have in mind.

## A quick checklist before you buy
- Check price history for the last one to three months, not just the last day
- Compare the identical model, storage variant, and color across at least two platforms
- Confirm whether the discount is off MRP or off a recent real selling price
- Read the fine print on any bank card or coupon requirement in the headline price
- Ignore stock counters and timers entirely when deciding

None of this takes long once it becomes a habit, and it turns "is this a good deal" from a guess into something you can verify in the time it takes the page to load. Do it three or four times and spotting an inflated discount stops needing a checklist at all, it just becomes obvious on sight. A minute of checking beats trusting a red banner on the listing page.
