I've been on the other side of this table more than once, interviewing freshers for my team, and the pattern surprised me the first time I saw it: a candidate who solves a problem in total silence and gets it right often scores worse than one who talks through a partially-correct approach. Technical interviews are testing whether you can think out loud through something unfamiliar, not whether you've memorized the answer key. Prepare for that.

## Week 1 — Fundamentals

[IMAGE: Timeline showing the four-week interview prep plan: Fundamentals, Problem Patterns, Core CS Subjects, Mock Interviews]
- Data structures: arrays, strings, linked lists, stacks, queues, hash maps
- Time and space complexity — be able to explain Big O for anything you write, not just recite it
- Pick one language and get fluent in its standard library (sorting, string methods, common built-ins)

## Week 2 — Problem Patterns, Not Random Problems
Instead of grinding problems at random, learn the recurring patterns: two pointers, sliding window, binary search, BFS/DFS on trees and graphs, basic dynamic programming. Most interview problems are a variation of one of these — recognizing which pattern applies is the real skill.

## Week 3 — Core CS Subjects
- Operating systems: processes vs threads, deadlocks, memory management basics
- DBMS: normalization, indexes, joins, ACID properties, basic SQL
- Networks: OSI basics, TCP vs UDP, what happens when you type a URL and hit enter
- OOP: encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, with examples you can explain, not just define

## Week 4 — Mock Interviews and Your Own Projects
Run 3-5 mock interviews with a friend, senior, or online platform before the real thing. Prepare to discuss 1-2 of your own projects in depth — being unable to explain your own resume in detail is one of the fastest ways freshers lose credibility.

## The Six Steps of Answering a Coding Question
- Repeat the problem back in your own words to confirm you understood it
- Ask about edge cases and constraints before writing any code
- Say your approach out loud before coding it, so the interviewer can redirect you early if needed
- Write clean code, narrating as you go
- Test your solution against edge cases yourself before calling it done
- State the time and space complexity of your final solution

## What Costs Freshers Offers That Has Nothing to Do With Coding

### Going silent while thinking
Interviewers can't evaluate a thought process they can't hear.

### Skipping clarifying questions
Jumping straight into code without confirming input format or edge cases reads as impulsive, not confident.

### Arguing with a hint
A nudge from the interviewer is usually them trying to help you succeed, not a challenge to defend your original approach.

### Having no questions at the end
"Any questions for us?" is part of the evaluation. Prepare two or three genuine ones about the team or tech stack.

## The Behavioral Round Matters As Much As the Coding Round
Freshers often treat the HR or behavioral round as a formality after clearing the technical rounds — that's a mistake. Interviewers use it to check whether you'll work well on a team, and it's scored, not just a formality. Prepare answers using the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Have two or three real examples ready — a group project that went wrong and how you handled it, a time you disagreed with a teammate, a deadline you nearly missed. Vague answers like "I'm a hard worker" don't hold up against a specific, concrete story.

## Basic System Design, Even for Fresher Roles
Full system design interviews are rare for freshers, but a light version increasingly shows up: "How would you design a URL shortener?" or "How would you structure a simple e-commerce database?" You're not expected to design something production-ready. Interviewers want to see whether you can break an ambiguous problem into components — what data needs storing, how pieces talk to each other, what happens at scale — rather than freezing because the question doesn't have one correct answer.

## How the HR Round Differs From the Technical Rounds
The HR round isn't testing knowledge — it's testing fit and communication. Common questions include why you want this specific company (a generic answer here is an immediate red flag — research the company beforehand), what you know about the role, and salary expectations. Have a researched number ready rather than an evasive non-answer; check ranges on sites like AmbitionBox or Glassdoor for similar roles before you walk in.

## Practice Platforms Worth Using
LeetCode and HackerRank cover the problem-pattern practice from Week 2. For mock interviews specifically, Pramp and interviewing.io pair you with another candidate or an engineer for a live, timed practice round — closer to real interview pressure than solving problems alone at your desk. Neither replaces genuine practice with a friend who can push back and ask follow-up questions the way a real interviewer would.

## A Mistake That Costs More Than a Wrong Answer
Watch out for this pattern: a candidate solves a problem correctly, then loses the offer anyway — because when asked "can you make this faster," they get defensive instead of engaging with the question. Interviewers ask follow-ups to see how you respond to pushback, not because your first answer was wrong. Treating every follow-up as a challenge to defend, instead of a genuine question to explore, is one of the more avoidable ways freshers lose offers they were otherwise on track for.

## What to Do the Night Before
Re-read the job description and your own resume — not new material. Sleep matters more than one more practice problem; a tired brain performs worse on both coding and communication than a rested one that's slightly less "prepared." Lay out what you're wearing, confirm the interview link or address, and stop studying by early evening.

## When You Genuinely Don't Know the Answer
Say so. Then think out loud about how you'd go about finding it: related concepts you do know, how you'd start breaking the problem down. A blank stare is worse than an honest "I haven't seen this exact problem before, but here's where I'd start." And on mock interviews, three to five is usually enough to work through the nerves and spot your own recurring mistakes. Communication problems especially don't show up until someone's watching you solve a problem in real time.

## Researching the Company Actually Matters
Spend twenty minutes on the company's engineering blog, recent product launches, or news before the interview — not to memorize trivia, but to ask one specific, informed question during the interview instead of a generic one. "What's your engineering team's biggest technical challenge right now" reads very differently coming from someone who clearly did fifteen minutes of homework versus someone asking it as a default script.
None of this requires natural confidence — it requires preparation specific enough that confidence follows from knowing what you're walking into.

## Why Explaining Your Thought Process Out Loud Feels Unnatural at First
Narrating a thought process while solving a problem is a different skill from solving the problem itself, and most people have never practiced doing both simultaneously before their first real interview, which is exactly why it feels awkward and slow in early mock interviews. That awkwardness fades with repetition, not with getting technically better at the problems themselves, which is why mock interview volume matters as much as raw practice problem count in the weeks leading up to a real interview.

## What to Do If You Freeze Completely Mid-Interview
Freezing happens even to well-prepared candidates, and the recovery matters more than avoiding it entirely. Saying plainly "give me a moment to think through this" and taking a genuine pause, rather than filling the silence with panicked, unstructured talking, reads as composure rather than weakness to an interviewer who has seen this exact moment many times before and generally respects a candidate who handles it calmly.

## Why Dressing and Setting Up Properly for a Video Interview Still Matters
For remote technical interviews specifically, testing your camera, microphone, and internet connection the day before, along with picking a quiet, well-lit spot, removes a category of avoidable friction that has nothing to do with technical skill but can still visibly rattle a candidate right at the start of the conversation, before a single question has even been asked.

## What to Bring Physically to an In-Person Interview
Printed copies of your resume, a notebook and pen, and a list of your own questions written down beforehand — small things, but showing up without them reads as underprepared before you've answered a single question. Interviewers notice who came ready to take notes and who didn't, and it's one of the easiest impressions to control before a single technical question gets asked, entirely within your own hands the night before, unlike the technical questions themselves, which no amount of preparation the night before can fully predict.

## The Real Test
Four focused weeks — fundamentals, patterns, core CS, and mock practice — will put you ahead of most freshers grinding problems without a plan. In my experience sitting across the table, structured thinking and clear communication are worth more than raw memorization, every single time. Four focused weeks beats months of scattered, directionless practice.
