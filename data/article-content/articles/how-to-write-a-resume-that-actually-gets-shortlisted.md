## Why Most Resumes Get Skipped in Six Seconds
I've screened resumes for open roles on my own team, and it really is that fast. Recruiters aren't reading your resume line by line on the first pass — they're scanning for evidence you can do the job, fast. A resume full of duty descriptions ("responsible for," "worked on," "helped with") gives them nothing to latch onto. A resume full of specific outcomes does.

## The Structure Recruiters Scan For
- Header — name, phone, email, LinkedIn, location (city is enough, skip the full address)
- Summary (2-3 lines, optional but useful) — your role, years of experience, and one standout achievement
- Experience — most recent first, 3-5 outcome-based bullets per role
- Skills — specific tools and technologies, not vague traits like "hardworking" or "team player"
- Education — degree, institution, year; below experience unless you're a fresher

## Quantify Everything You Can
Numbers don't have to be dramatic to work. "Managed a team of 4" is a number. "Reduced errors by 15%" is a number. If you can't quantify something, describe the scope instead — "managed the onboarding process for all new hires" still gives more to grab onto than "helped with onboarding."

## What to Cut
- An objective statement that just says you want the job — every applicant wants the job, it adds nothing
- Skills you haven't used in years or can't speak to confidently in an interview
- Full sentences with "I" — resume bullets are fragments starting with an action verb, not sentences
- A photo, unless it's standard practice in your specific industry or country

## Getting Past Applicant Tracking Systems
Many companies run resumes through software that scans for keywords from the job description before a human sees them. Mirror the exact terms the posting uses (if it says "cross-functional collaboration," don't just write "teamwork"), keep formatting simple (no tables or text boxes that scanning software can misread), and save as a PDF unless the application asks for a Word document.

## Mistakes That Get Resumes Rejected Instantly
- Typos and inconsistent formatting — reads as carelessness before anyone reads a single bullet
- A generic resume sent to every job instead of one tailored to each posting's actual requirements
- Listing responsibilities in past roles that don't connect to the job you're applying for now
- Going over two pages for anything under 10 years of experience

## Tailoring Without Rewriting From Scratch Every Time
A fully custom resume for every single application isn't realistic, but a five-minute pass before each submission is: swap the summary line to match the role's language, reorder bullets so the most relevant ones for that specific job sit at the top of each role, and drop or shrink experience that has nothing to do with the position. This gets most of the benefit of full customization for a fraction of the time.

## The Cover Letter Question
Many applications mark it optional, and most applicants skip it — which is exactly why a specific, non-generic cover letter stands out. A short one referencing something concrete about the role or company, rather than a templated "I am writing to express my interest," is worth the extra ten minutes when the application allows one, even if it's optional.

## Resume Length: When Two Pages Makes Sense
The one-page rule is a starting guideline, not an absolute law — someone with 8+ years of relevant experience and multiple roles worth detailing can reasonably run to two pages, as long as every line still earns its place. The test isn't the page count itself, it's whether cutting further would remove relevant evidence of capability.

## Two Questions I Get Asked a Lot
Should a fresher with no work experience include a skills section? Yes, definitely. For freshers, the skills section, paired with a strong projects section, does the same work that an experience section would for someone further along, and for technical roles it often carries more weight than education does. And how far back should work experience go? Generally the last 10 to 15 years. Older roles either get dropped entirely or condensed into a single line, since recruiters weight recent, relevant experience far more heavily than anything from over a decade ago.

## Formatting Choices That Actually Matter
Stick to one font, one font size for body text (10-11pt is standard), and consistent spacing throughout — visual inconsistency reads as carelessness even when the content itself is strong. Use bold sparingly, for role titles or standout numbers, not entire sentences; overusing it defeats the purpose of drawing the eye anywhere specific. Save as a PDF with a clear filename like "FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf" rather than the default "Resume_final_v3.pdf" your file manager suggests.

## What Recruiters Look for in the First Pass
Beyond the outcome-based bullets covered above, recruiters scan for a stable-looking career progression (or a clear, reasonable explanation for gaps), title and seniority appropriate to the role applied for, and keyword overlap with the posting's core requirements. None of this means gaming the resume — it means making relevant experience easy to find in six seconds, since that's roughly all the time a first pass gets.

## Handling Employment Gaps Without Drawing Extra Attention to Them
A short, honest gap, a few months between roles, generally doesn't need explanation on the resume itself, since a cover letter or interview is the better place for that context if it comes up at all. A longer gap is better addressed with a brief, factual line rather than left as an unexplained blank a recruiter has to guess about, since an unexplained gap tends to generate more suspicion than a plainly stated reason ever would, even when the actual reason is completely unremarkable.

## Why LinkedIn and Resume Consistency Matters More Than People Realize
Recruiters frequently open a candidate's LinkedIn profile alongside the resume, and a noticeable mismatch in job titles, dates, or described responsibilities between the two documents raises a credibility question before the recruiter has even reached the interview stage. Keeping the core facts identical across both, updated at the same time whenever one changes, avoids an entirely avoidable red flag that has nothing to do with actual qualifications.

## Why the Same Resume Rarely Works for Two Different Roles
A resume optimized for a backend engineering role won't score well against a product management posting, even at the same company, because the keywords, the emphasis, and the achievements that matter differ entirely. Keeping two or three base versions for the role types you're applying to saves more time than customizing from one generic master copy every single time.

## What to Put in a Reference Section, If You Include One
"References available upon request" is a filler line every recruiter already assumes is true and doesn't need to be stated explicitly — it wastes a line that could instead carry another quantified achievement. If specific references are requested by an application, provide them as a separate document rather than cluttering the resume itself with contact details nobody will look at during the initial screening pass.

## A Final Thought on Why This Matters More Than It Seems
A resume isn't the whole hiring decision, but it's the gate everything else has to pass through first, and a strong resume can get someone with slightly less experience an interview over someone stronger on paper but harder to read.

## Why a Second Reader Catches What You Can't
After staring at your own resume for hours, typos and awkward phrasing become invisible — your brain fills in what it expects to see. Hand it to someone outside your field and ask them to summarize what you do in one sentence after a sixty-second read. If they can't, a recruiter skimming for six seconds definitely won't. This test costs nothing and catches problems no amount of self-editing will, since you're too close to your own work to see it the way a stranger reading it cold does, no matter how many times you personally proofread it.

## Before You Hit Submit
Read every bullet and ask: does this prove I can do the job, or just describe what I did? If it's the second one, add a number or an outcome. It's a small check, but it's the one I look for first when I'm the one reading, and it fixes most resumes.
