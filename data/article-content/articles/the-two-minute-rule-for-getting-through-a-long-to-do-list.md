Open a to-do list app on any given day and you'll usually find a mess of unrelated items stacked on top of each other — a five-second reply next to a two-hour report next to a task you've been avoiding for a week. The two-minute rule is a simple filter for that mess: can this be done in two minutes or less? If yes, do it right now instead of writing it down. If no, it goes onto the real list.

## Why the two-minute cutoff works
Two minutes is short enough that almost nothing you'd batch or schedule takes less time to just do than to process as a task. Replying to a one-line message, filing a receipt, updating a spreadsheet cell — the overhead of adding these to a list, remembering them, and revisiting them later usually costs more time than the task itself. The cutoff isn't arbitrary politeness; it's a break-even point where 'do it now' becomes objectively faster than 'manage it as a task.'

## Run the filter before you plan the day, not during it
Apply the rule as a first pass over the entire list, before you start working, rather than deciding item-by-item while you're mid-task. Go down the list once, fast, and do every sub-two-minute item as you hit it. This typically clears a third to half of a long list in under ten minutes, leaving a much shorter list of things that need focused time — which is the list you plan your day around.

## Watch for the trap: using it to avoid real work
The two-minute rule has a failure mode where it becomes a way to feel productive while avoiding the one hard task on the list. If you notice yourself finding an unusual number of 'quick' two-minute items right when a difficult task is due, that's avoidance, not efficiency. The rule is for small items, not for manufacturing busywork. A rough test: if you're not sure whether something is really under two minutes, time it once — you'll usually be surprised which way it goes.

## What doesn't belong in the two-minute pass
Tasks that are individually quick but recurring, like responding to every notification the moment it arrives, shouldn't be run through this rule constantly throughout the day, because that turns a list-clearing technique into a source of constant interruption. Apply it once per list review, not as a live filter on every incoming ping. Also exclude anything that requires someone else's input to finish; 'send a message asking X' is two minutes, but the underlying task isn't done until they reply, so it still needs a placeholder on the real list.

## Applying the Rule to Digital Versus Physical Tasks Differently
A two-minute digital task — replying to a message, updating a calendar entry — has essentially zero setup cost. A two-minute physical task, filing a document, putting something away, often has a hidden setup and cleanup cost around it that makes the two-minute estimate optimistic. Worth being slightly more generous with the cutoff for physical tasks specifically, to avoid consistently underestimating them.

## What to Do When the List Itself Feels Overwhelming to Even Open
If dread about the length of a list is what's stopping you from starting the two-minute pass at all, set a timer for five minutes and commit only to that, not to finishing the whole list. Starting the mechanical sorting process is usually enough to break the avoidance, and most people keep going past the timer once they're in motion.

## Why This Rule Pairs Well With Batching Larger Tasks Separately
Everything that survives the two-minute filter still needs a home beyond a single flat list. Grouping the remaining items by rough size or type — quick calls, focused work, follow-ups needed from someone else — before scheduling them gives a clearer sense of how the rest of the day should be structured, instead of working through a leftover list in whatever order it happens to be sorted.

## A Note on Doing This With a Team's Shared List
Applying the same two-minute filter to a shared team task board works the same way individually, but it's worth a quick check-in before mass-completing items that affect someone else's workflow, since a task that looks like a two-minute close from your side might still need a heads-up to the person who assigned it.

## Combine it with a simple two-list setup
Keep a Today list and a Later list. Run the two-minute filter against Later first thing, clearing what you can, then move anything left that's time-sensitive into Today. Over a few weeks this keeps Later from becoming a graveyard of small unfinished tasks that make the whole list look worse than it is.
Run this filter once before you plan your day and you'll usually find the list is far more manageable than it looked at first glance. The small stuff gets handled in minutes, and what's left is a short, honest list of the things that need real time and attention.
