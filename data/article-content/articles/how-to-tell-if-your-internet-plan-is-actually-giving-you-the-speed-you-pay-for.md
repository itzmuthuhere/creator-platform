Downloads on your 200 Mbps plan feel more like 90 Mbps, and the instinct is to call your ISP and start arguing. Do the test properly first — a badly run speed test produces a number that has nothing to do with what your connection is capable of.

## Test with a wired connection first
Wi-Fi has its own bottlenecks — distance from the router, interference, how many devices are connected, the capability of your phone or laptop's Wi-Fi chip. If you test over Wi-Fi and get 90 Mbps on a 200 Mbps plan, you don't yet know if the problem is your ISP or your Wi-Fi. Connect a laptop directly to the router with an Ethernet cable and run the test again. If the wired number is close to what you're paying for, your ISP is fine and the problem is your Wi-Fi setup, not your plan.

## Test at different times of day
Run the test at least three times: mid-morning, evening around 8-10pm when most households in your area are streaming, and late night. ISPs oversubscribe their local network capacity, meaning the pipe serving your building or street is shared. If speeds tank during peak evening hours and recover late at night, that's a sign of local network congestion rather than your individual line being throttled.

## Use more than one speed test tool
Speed test sites connect you to a nearby server, and results can vary depending on which server you land on and how busy it is. Run the test on at least two different services rather than trusting one single result. Also try your ISP's own speed test tool if they provide one — an ISP's own speed-test tool may not be fully independent, so cross-check it against a third-party test.

## Understand what your plan number means
The number your ISP advertises is almost always download speed, and it's usually described as "up to." Upload speed on many home broadband plans is much lower than download, which matters a lot if you're on video calls or uploading large files for work. Check your plan's fine print for the actual upload speed committed, and test that separately — most speed test tools show both numbers.

## Rule out your own devices and router capability
An older router bought years ago may simply not be capable of handling your current plan's speed, especially if you've upgraded your plan since buying it. Check the router's specification sheet for its maximum throughput. Similarly, a laptop with an old Wi-Fi chip, or a phone connected to 2.4GHz instead of 5GHz, will cap out well below what a fast plan can deliver, and that's not your ISP's fault.

## What to do if the numbers don't add up
If your wired, off-peak, cross-checked test consistently comes in significantly below your plan speed, that's a legitimate case to raise with your ISP's support. Have the test results ready — screenshots with date, time, and connection type (wired vs Wi-Fi) — since a vague "internet is slow" complaint is much easier for support to brush off than a documented pattern. Many ISPs in India will send a technician to check line quality if you can show a clear, repeated shortfall.

## Why the Number Might Differ by Device Even on the Same Wi-Fi
An older phone or laptop with an outdated Wi-Fi chip will show a lower speed test result than a newer device on the exact same network at the exact same time. Testing on more than one device before concluding your internet plan is underperforming rules out a device-specific limitation instead of a genuine ISP shortfall.

## What Contention Ratio Actually Means for Your Real-World Speed
ISPs sell bandwidth based on a contention ratio — your advertised speed is a maximum, not a guaranteed constant, shared to some degree with other users on the same local infrastructure. This is standard practice across the industry, not unique to any one provider, but it does explain why speeds can legitimately vary through the day even when nothing is technically wrong with your connection.

## Checking Whether the Slowdown Is the Website, Not Your Connection
A single website or streaming service loading slowly while a speed test shows full speed usually points to an issue on that service's end — a busy server, regional routing — not your internet connection at all. Testing a different site or service when something feels slow helps isolate whether the problem is yours to fix or someone else's.

## What a Documented Pattern Gets You From Support
ISP support teams are far more likely to escalate to a technician visit or a genuine account-level fix when shown a week of consistent, properly documented shortfall data instead of a single complaint. Treat it as a short investigation, not a one-time check, and the odds of an actual resolution go up instead of getting a generic troubleshooting script.

## Why Comparing Notes With Neighbors Is a Useful Extra Check
If several households on the same street or in the same building, ideally on the same ISP, are all seeing a similar shortfall during the same peak hours, that's a stronger signal of local infrastructure congestion than any single household's test results alone, and it's also a more effective way to get an ISP's attention, since a documented multi-household pattern is harder to dismiss as one customer's router problem than a single, easily-brushed-off complaint.

## Checking Whether a VPN or Background App Is Quietly Eating Bandwidth
A VPN connection, a large background app update, or a cloud backup running silently on another device connected to the same network can all consume meaningful bandwidth during a test without being obvious at a glance, which is why briefly disconnecting other devices and pausing anything obviously data-heavy before running a proper test isolates whether the shortfall is the connection itself or simply competition for the same pipe from something you'd forgotten was running.

## Reading the Fine Print on Fair Usage Policy Limits
Some broadband plans, particularly older or budget-tier ones, throttle speed after a certain monthly data threshold is crossed, a detail that's easy to miss when signing up but explains a sudden, sustained slowdown partway through a billing cycle that has nothing to do with network congestion or your own equipment at all. Checking your plan's fair usage policy details directly on the ISP's website clarifies whether this applies before assuming the connection itself has degraded for some other reason entirely.

## Why Peak-Hour Testing Alone Isn't the Full Picture
Testing only during the busiest evening hours can make a perfectly fine connection look worse than it is if that specific hour happens to coincide with a temporary local issue. Spread tests across a full week, not just one bad evening, before drawing a firm conclusion either way.

## When Switching ISPs Is Genuinely the Right Call
If a documented, properly tested shortfall persists across multiple support tickets and technician visits with no real improvement, at some point the more efficient move is simply switching to a different provider serving the same area rather than continuing to chase a fix that isn't materializing, particularly now that porting a broadband connection has become considerably less painful than it used to be in most Indian cities.

## A Simple Monthly Habit Worth Keeping
Running one quick wired speed test on the same day each month, logged in a simple note, builds a baseline over time that makes any future slowdown obvious immediately instead of something you only notice once it's already gotten bad enough to be frustrating, and it gives you ready evidence the moment you need to raise a complaint.

## Checking Speed During a Video Call, Not Just a Download Test
A standard speed test measures raw throughput, but a video call stresses upload speed and connection stability in a way a download test doesn't capture at all. If calls freeze or drop while a speed test shows a strong number, run a dedicated video-call quality test, several browser-based tools exist for this, or simply note the exact time a call degrades and compare it against your logged speed tests from around that time.

## What an ISP's Own App Won't Tell You
Many providers' own apps show a status page claiming everything is fine even during a genuine outage affecting your specific area, since that status often reflects the network's general health rather than your individual connection. Treat a green status indicator as a starting point, not proof, especially if your own repeated, carefully run tests consistently disagree with it.

## Why Two Devices in the Same Room Can Show Different Results
Wi-Fi antenna quality varies between phones, laptops, and smart TVs, so a weak result on one device in a room with strong signal elsewhere in the house often points to that specific device's hardware, not the router or the plan. Testing on your best device first rules this out before blaming the connection itself.

## The bigger picture
Twenty minutes of proper testing — wired, more than one time of day, more than one tool — is the difference between a complaint your ISP takes seriously and one they wave off as a router problem on your end.
