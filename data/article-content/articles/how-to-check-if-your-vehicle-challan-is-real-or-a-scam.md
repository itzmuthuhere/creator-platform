Fake challan SMS messages tend to follow the same script: an urgent tone, a small amount that feels easier to just pay than question, and a link that doesn't lead to a government domain. The safest habit is simple — never pay through a link sent in a text message, and verify independently instead.

## Never click the link in the SMS directly
Genuine e-challans can be checked and paid without ever clicking a link sent to you. If the message contains a link to a domain that isn't parivahan.gov.in, or a state transport department's official domain, or ends in something unusual like .xyz, .top, or a shortened URL, treat it as suspicious by default. Fraudulent challan messages are one of the more common phishing formats because a small traffic fine feels like something worth paying quickly without scrutiny.

## Verify directly on the Parivahan portal
Go to echallan.parivahan.gov.in and use the Check Challan Status option. You can search either by challan number, if the SMS gave you one, or by your vehicle registration number, or by your driving licence number. If a real challan exists against your vehicle, it will show up here with the issuing authority, date, offence, and amount — matching or not matching what the SMS claimed.

## What it means if nothing shows up
If you search by vehicle number and no matching challan appears at all, the SMS is almost certainly fake. Legitimate challans issued by traffic police or through automated cameras are uploaded to this central system, typically within a short window of being issued — a message referencing a challan number that returns nothing on the official portal has no legal basis behind it.

## Cross-check with your state's own traffic portal
Some states also run their own traffic e-challan portals in addition to the central Parivahan system — for instance, Delhi Traffic Police and Mumbai Traffic Police both have dedicated challan lookup pages. If you're unsure, checking on both the central Parivahan portal and your state's portal gives you a second confirmation point, especially since integration timing between state systems and the central database can occasionally lag.

## Signs that give away a fake challan message
- A payment link that isn't the parivahan.gov.in domain or a recognizable state government domain
- Pressure to pay within a very short deadline, often hours
- A request to pay via a personal UPI ID rather than the standard government payment gateway
- Spelling errors or a generic greeting rather than your actual vehicle number in the message
- An SMS from a regular 10-digit mobile number rather than an official short code

## If you confirm the challan is real
Pay it only through echallan.parivahan.gov.in directly, using the official payment gateway integrated there, which supports UPI, cards, and net banking through a secure government-linked checkout, not a third-party link sent over SMS.

## Why Genuine Challans Sometimes Take Time to Appear Online
A challan issued manually by a traffic officer on the spot can take a day or two to reflect on the central Parivahan portal, since it needs to be entered into the system after the fact, while camera-detected challans tend to upload faster since the process is more automated. If an SMS references a challan that doesn't show up yet, waiting a day and checking again is more sensible than assuming it's automatically fake, though the core rule against paying via a text link still applies either way.

## What to Do If You Get a Call Instead of an SMS
Fraud attempts increasingly use a phone call instead of a text message — someone claiming to be traffic police and asking for immediate UPI payment over the call itself. Real traffic enforcement in India does not collect fines by asking you to make a UPI payment to a personal number over a phone call. Treating any such call as fraudulent by default, and verifying independently on the Parivahan portal, is the correct response regardless of how official the caller sounds.

## Keeping a Record for Vehicles You Rarely Drive
For a vehicle used infrequently — a second car, an inherited vehicle — a periodic challan check every few months, even without receiving any SMS at all, catches genuine challans, camera-detected offenses in particular, that might otherwise go unnoticed and accumulate late fees or escalate to a court notice if left unaddressed for long enough.

## Setting Up Alerts So You Don't Have to Check Manually
Some state transport portals and third-party apps offer an alert or notification service tied to your vehicle registration number, worth exploring once you've confirmed it links to an official source, since a proactive alert removes the need to remember to check periodically and closes the same gap a fake-SMS scammer is trying to exploit through urgency in the first place.

## Why the Amount Requested Is Often a Deliberate Choice
Fraudulent challan messages tend to request amounts in a specific range, large enough to be worth the scammer's effort but small enough that most people would rather just pay than spend time verifying it, typically somewhere between two hundred and a couple of thousand rupees. Recognizing that this amount is a calculated psychological choice, not a coincidence, is itself a useful piece of context the next time a message like this arrives asking for quick payment, since scammers have learned exactly what threshold makes people pause versus just pay and move on with their day.

## Checking a Challan Right After a Trip, Not Months Later
Doing a quick portal check within a week or two of a longer drive, rather than waiting for an SMS that may or may not arrive promptly, catches a genuine camera-issued challan early enough to dispute it if something looks wrong.

## What a Genuine Challan SMS Actually Looks Like
A real e-challan notification typically comes from a registered short code rather than a regular ten-digit mobile number, references your actual vehicle registration number correctly rather than a generic greeting, and directs you toward the official Parivahan domain rather than a shortened or unfamiliar link. Comparing any suspicious message against these specific traits, rather than relying on a vague gut feeling alone, gives a more reliable first read before you even get to the portal verification step, and it's a comparison that takes seconds once you know what to look for in the message itself.

## Checking Multiple Vehicles Under One Family Account
Households managing several vehicles, a car and two-wheelers across different family members, benefit from checking all registration numbers together during a periodic review rather than only reacting to whichever vehicle happens to receive a suspicious message, since a genuine challan on a rarely-driven second vehicle can otherwise go unnoticed for months and accumulate late fees in the meantime.

## Why Sharing This Habit With Older Family Members Matters Specifically
Older relatives who drive but are less familiar with checking things online are disproportionately targeted by exactly this kind of scam, since a phone call demanding urgent payment plays more convincingly on someone less comfortable navigating a government portal to verify independently. A short, patient walkthrough of the Parivahan check once, done together in person, tends to stick far better than a general warning to "be careful."

## A Simple Rule That Covers Almost Every Version of This Scam
If a message about money arrives with pressure to act fast and a link that isn't a domain you already trust, the response is always the same regardless of what it claims to be about: don't click, verify independently through the official channel, then act — that one habit alone defeats nearly every variation of this scam regardless of how convincing the specific details look.

## Why Some States Show Challans Faster Than Others
Integration between state traffic police systems and the central Parivahan database varies in speed depending on the state. Some states sync within hours; others take a day or two. A message referencing a challan that hasn't appeared yet on the central portal isn't automatically fake — check your specific state's own traffic portal too before assuming the worst, since it may simply be ahead of the central sync.

## Why Officers Sometimes Issue a Challan Without Telling You Directly
Camera-based enforcement issues a large share of challans without any officer present at all, meaning the first you hear of it is often the SMS itself, not a roadside stop. This is completely normal and doesn't make the challan less real — it's simply how automated enforcement works, which is exactly why checking the portal directly matters more than judging a message by whether it matches your memory of the day.

## What a Genuine Payment Confirmation Looks Like
After paying a real challan through the official portal, you'll get a receipt with a specific payment reference number, not just a generic thank-you message. Save that receipt; it's your proof of payment if the same challan somehow shows as unpaid later due to a processing delay on the department's end.

## If you've already paid a fake one
Report it right away at cybercrime.gov.in or by calling 1930, the same channel you'd use for any UPI fraud, and hold on to the transaction reference and the original SMS as evidence. The sooner it's reported, the better the odds of the receiving account being frozen before the money moves further.
