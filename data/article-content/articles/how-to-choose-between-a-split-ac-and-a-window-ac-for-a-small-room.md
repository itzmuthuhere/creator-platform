Split or window — the question resurfaces every year right before summer, and there's no universally correct answer to it. What decides it is your specific room: the wall you have, the noise you can tolerate, and the budget you're working with, not a general preference for one type over the other.

## The core mechanical difference
A window AC has everything — compressor, condenser, evaporator — in a single unit that sits in a window or a wall opening, with the hot side facing outward and the cool side facing into the room. A split AC separates this: the noisy compressor and condenser sit outside as a separate outdoor unit, connected by pipes to an indoor unit mounted on the wall that only handles cooling and blowing air. This one structural difference explains most of the practical trade-offs below.

## Installation constraints
Window ACs need an actual window or a wall opening of the right size and shape, and they need to be structurally supported since the full unit's weight sits in that opening. If you don't have a suitable window, or live in an apartment where the building doesn't allow wall openings, a window AC simply isn't an option, and a split becomes the only real choice. Split ACs need space outside for the outdoor unit — a balcony, a dedicated ledge, or a common external wall — plus permission if you're in a rented flat or a building with strict façade rules, since the outdoor unit and pipe routing changes the building's exterior.

## Noise
This is one of the clearer differences. Because the compressor is inside the room in a window AC, it's noticeably noisier in the room where you're sleeping or working, even in decent quality units. A split AC's compressor noise stays outside, so the indoor unit is much quieter, which matters more than people expect once they've lived with a window AC in a bedroom for a season.

## Cooling and look
For a small room, both types in a matched tonnage will cool similarly well — tonnage and star rating matter more for cooling performance than the type itself. Splits are generally considered better looking, since only a slim indoor unit is visible in the room, while a window AC visibly occupies part of a window or wall. If your room has a window you don't mind losing natural light through, or you want the lowest upfront cost and don't mind the noise, window AC is a reasonable, practical choice.

## Sizing for a small room
For a small bedroom (roughly 100-120 sq ft), a 1-ton unit is usually sufficient in either type, though a room with heavy sun exposure, more than 2 occupants regularly, or poor insulation may need a slightly higher capacity. Oversizing wastes money and can cool poorly due to short cycling, while undersizing means the unit runs constantly without adequately cooling the room — check a capacity guide or ask the dealer to size it against your specific room's dimensions and sun exposure rather than guessing.

## Energy Efficiency Differences Beyond Just the Star Rating
Inverter technology, now common in both window and split ACs, adjusts compressor speed continuously instead of switching fully on and off, using less electricity over a cooling season than older non-inverter units of the same star rating. Checking for inverter technology alongside the star rating gives a more complete picture of actual running cost than the star rating alone.

## What Happens During Installation That Buyers Don't Always Expect
Split AC installation involves drilling through an external wall for the pipe connection and mounting an outdoor unit securely, which takes noticeably longer and is more disruptive than a window AC's installation. Worth scheduling installation with enough lead time before peak summer, when installation teams get booked out weeks in advance.

## Servicing Costs to Factor Into the Comparison
Annual servicing costs for a split AC are typically somewhat higher than for a window AC, since a technician needs to service both the indoor and outdoor units and check the refrigerant line connecting them. This recurring cost difference is worth adding to the upfront price comparison, instead of treating the purchase price as the full picture of what each option costs over several years.

## A Third Option Worth Knowing About: Portable AC
For renters who can't install either a window unit or a split's outdoor component, a portable AC with an exhaust hose vented through a small gap in a window is a genuine third option, though it typically costs more to run for the same cooling output and is noisier than a split, since its compressor sits inside the room too. It's best thought of as a fallback for situations where neither standard option is physically possible, rather than a first choice on its own merits.

## Warranty Differences Worth Checking Before You Buy
Compressor warranties on both types typically run several years and are usually comparable across window and split units from the same brand and price tier, but installation-related warranty terms differ: a split AC's warranty can sometimes be voided by improper installation from an unauthorized technician, while a window AC's simpler, self-contained installation carries less of that risk. Always confirm who's doing the installation and whether they're brand-authorized before committing, particularly for a split.

## Apartment and Society Rules That Can Decide This for You
Some housing societies and newer apartment complexes have specific rules restricting where outdoor AC units can be mounted, sometimes requiring a designated AC ledge rather than any external wall, and a few restrict window-mounted units entirely for façade consistency. Checking your specific building's rules before purchase avoids buying a unit you then can't legally install where you intended.

## Running Cost Over a Few Years, Not Just the Purchase Price
Because a split AC's compressor sits outside and away from insulation losses at the window frame, and modern splits lean more heavily toward inverter technology, a split often ends up cheaper to run over three to five years of regular use even though it costs more upfront, particularly in regions with long, hot summers where the AC runs for months at a stretch. For a room used only occasionally, the upfront cost difference matters more than the smaller running-cost gap; for a room used nightly through a full summer, the calculation shifts toward the split.

## How Noise Regulations Sometimes Affect the Outdoor Unit Placement
Some municipal bodies and housing societies enforce noise limits on outdoor compressor placement relative to a neighbor's window, particularly in densely packed apartment buildings, which can restrict exactly where a split's outdoor unit is allowed to sit even when balcony or ledge space is technically available. Confirming this with your building management before finalizing installation avoids a scenario where the unit gets installed and then needs to be relocated due to a neighbor's complaint.

## Why Capacity Guides Alone Aren't Always Enough
Standard tonnage recommendations assume fairly typical room conditions, and rooms with unusually high ceilings, large uncovered glass windows facing direct afternoon sun, or poor wall insulation from older construction often need a size up from what a generic sq ft chart suggests. Asking an installer to do a quick heat-load assessment specific to your room, rather than relying purely on floor area, avoids the common mistake of undersizing a unit for a room that looks small on paper but traps more heat than average.

## Don't Skip the Basic Trial Run
Before final wall-mounting or wiring in a rented flat, if the landlord permits it, running the unit temporarily to confirm it cools the room to your expectation avoids discovering a mismatch only after full installation is complete.

## Getting a Second Opinion From an Independent Technician
Many dealers naturally lean toward recommending whichever type they have more stock of or a better margin on, so getting a sizing and type recommendation from an independent AC technician, someone not tied to a specific brand's sales incentive, before committing to a purchase gives a more objective read on what fits your specific room and building constraints.

## A Quick Recap of the Decision Points
Wall or window opening available, budget-conscious, and comfortable with more compressor noise in the room: window AC. Outdoor space available, quieter nights the priority, and willing to pay more upfront and for servicing: split AC. Neither space option available at all: portable AC as the fallback, accepting the noise and running-cost tradeoff that comes with it, and treating it as a temporary solution rather than a permanent one if a proper split or window installation becomes possible later.

## Bottom line for a small room
Renting with no ability to touch the building's exterior, or simply prioritising the lowest total cost — window AC remains a perfectly sensible call despite the noise. Outdoor space available and sleep quality the higher priority — split earns its extra cost, particularly in a room you're sleeping in every night.
