A ₹149 charge showed up on a friend's bank statement last month for an app he'd deleted back in March. He assumed deleting it had ended things — instead the mandate kept firing every 30 days, quietly, because nothing had told the bank or Google Play to stop. This happens constantly: uninstalling wipes the app off your phone, not the standing authorization to charge you, and that authorization sits in one of a few very specific places — Google Play, the App Store, a UPI Autopay mandate, or a card token saved directly with the merchant. Finding and killing it at that source is the only thing that stops the money moving.

## Why cancellation is intentionally harder than sign-up
Sign-up is one tap because the company wants the conversion. Cancellation routes you through a settings menu three levels deep, or a "pause" offer, or a support chat, because every day you stay subscribed without noticing is revenue the company keeps without doing anything. This isn't a conspiracy theory — it's a well-documented UX pattern known as a "roach motel" design (easy in, hard out), and Indian apps use it as much as anyone else.

## Step 1: Find every subscription pulling money right now
You can't cancel what you can't see. Check three places, not one:
- Bank statement, last 3 months — sort by amount and look for repeating figures like ₹149, ₹499, ₹999 on the same date each month.
- UPI Autopay mandates — open your UPI app (GPay, PhonePe, Paytm) and go to the "Mandates" or "Autopay" section. This lists every recurring UPI authorization separately from your regular transaction history.
- Play Store / App Store subscriptions list — on Android, Play Store → profile icon → Payments & subscriptions → Subscriptions. On iPhone, Settings → your name → Subscriptions. This shows every app-billed subscription regardless of whether the app is still installed.

## Step 2: Cancel at the source, not in the app
If you subscribed through Play Store or the App Store, cancel from that list — cancelling inside the app itself often just logs you out, since Google or Apple owns the payment relationship, not the app developer. If you subscribed directly on a website with a card, cancel through that website's account settings, since the merchant owns the mandate. If it was set up as a UPI Autopay mandate, you can revoke it directly from your UPI app's Mandates section even if the merchant's own cancel flow is broken or hidden — the mandate is technically yours to control, not theirs.

## Step 3: Confirm it's dead
Don't trust a confirmation screen alone. Check a week later: the mandate should no longer show as "Active" in your UPI app, and no new authorization request should appear for the next billing date. A cancelled subscription won't send a payment request at all — if a debit request still pops up for approval, the merchant hasn't processed the cancellation, and you should reject the request and escalate.

## Platform-specific quirks worth knowing
Netflix, Hotstar, and similar OTT platforms let you cancel anytime, but you keep access until the paid period ends — that's normal, not a bug. Gym and insurance add-on subscriptions billed via NACH mandate (not UPI) need a separate cancellation request through your bank's NACH mandate cancellation form, which typically takes 3-5 working days to process, so cancel at least a week before the next due date.

## The Specific Trap of Free Trials That Auto-Convert
A large share of forgotten subscriptions start as a free trial signed up for a specific reason — a one-off document tool, a week of an OTT show — that nobody thought to cancel before the trial converted to a paid plan automatically. Setting a calendar reminder two days before any free trial ends, at the moment you sign up instead of trying to remember later, catches this specific pattern before it becomes a recurring charge at all.

## Why Family Sharing Plans Complicate the Audit
If a subscription is shared across a family plan, the person who set it up is often not the person still using it months later, and cancelling on their behalf without checking first can cut off someone else's access unexpectedly. Worth a quick message to whoever's on the plan before killing a shared subscription during a cleanup pass, rather than assuming nobody else still needs it.

## Why International or Overseas Subscriptions Are Especially Easy to Forget
Subscriptions billed in a foreign currency, a VPN service, an international streaming trial, or a course platform based abroad, often appear on a bank statement under an unfamiliar merchant name that doesn't obviously connect to the actual service, making them harder to spot during a quick statement scan. Searching the exact merchant name shown on the statement, rather than trying to recall it from memory, usually reveals what it is, and a quick web search of that exact string is often faster than trying to remember every service you've ever signed up for.

## Checking Your Email for Renewal Notices You Might Have Missed
Most subscription services send a renewal reminder email a few days before charging, and searching your inbox for terms like "renewal," "receipt," or "subscription" across the past year surfaces services you signed up for and then stopped thinking about, even ones that don't show up clearly in a bank statement scan because the amount seemed too small to notice at the time. This email search often turns up more forgotten subscriptions than the bank statement review alone.

## Free Tools That Can Help Automate Part of This Audit
Several personal finance and expense-tracking apps available in India can automatically flag recurring debit patterns from linked bank statements, which is useful as a first pass before manually confirming each one through the three-place check described earlier. Treat an app's automated flag as a starting point to investigate, not as confirmation a subscription is definitely still active, since these tools sometimes misclassify a one-off recurring bill, like a fixed EMI, as a subscription worth cancelling.

## What to Do If You Can't Identify a Recurring Charge at All
Occasionally a recurring charge shows up under a generic payment processor name that gives no clue what it's for. In that case, contacting your bank directly and asking them to identify the merchant behind the transaction reference is more reliable than guessing, and banks are generally able to trace this from their own transaction records even when the statement description itself is unhelpful, particularly for charges routed through a payment aggregator rather than billed directly by the merchant.

## Making This a Genuinely Recurring Habit, Not a One-Time Fix
New subscriptions creep back in constantly, a free trial started for one specific reason, an app that switched from one-time purchase to subscription during an update, a family member who added something to a shared payment method without mentioning it. Setting a recurring calendar reminder every six months to redo the full three-place audit, rather than treating this as a single cleanup you never need to repeat, catches new forgotten charges before they've had a chance to run for a full year.

## Why Annual Plans Deserve Extra Attention During This Audit
A subscription billed annually rather than monthly is far easier to forget, since a full year passes between charges instead of thirty days. Cross-check your bank statement against the exact same month from last year specifically, not just the recent months, to catch anything billed on this yearly cycle that a normal three-month lookback would completely miss.

## Why Family Members Should Know About Shared Subscriptions Too
If more than one person in your household has access to cancel a shared subscription, a quick heads-up before cancelling avoids a confusing moment where someone else discovers their access gone with no warning. A shared note listing what's currently active and who uses it prevents this kind of accidental disruption entirely, and takes only a few minutes to set up.

## What to Do If a Cancellation Confirmation Never Arrives
Some services process a cancellation without sending any confirmation at all, leaving you to wonder whether it went through. Taking a screenshot of the cancelled status shown inside the app or account settings, right after cancelling, gives you your own proof even without a confirmation email to fall back on if a dispute comes up later.

## If it still charges you after cancellation
Raise a dispute with your bank citing the mandate cancellation date and reference number — banks are required to act on cancelled UPI Autopay mandates, and a charge after a confirmed revocation is grounds to raise a formal dispute with your bank. Keep the cancellation confirmation as proof.
Run the three-place audit above once, and calendar a repeat every six months. Most people find at least one or two mandates they'd completely forgotten they were paying for — a ₹149 charge is easy to miss individually, but five of them running for a year adds up to real money. Run the audit once, then put a reminder on the calendar for six months out.
