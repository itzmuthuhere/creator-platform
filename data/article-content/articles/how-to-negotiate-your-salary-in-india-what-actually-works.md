My own offer of ₹8 LPA became ₹9.5 LPA after one email. Not through a dramatic ultimatum — through asking a specific question at the right point in the process. Most people leave money on the table not because they're bad negotiators, but because they never ask.

## Why Most People Don't Negotiate
The fear is usually that asking will get the offer pulled entirely. In practice, companies expect negotiation at the offer stage, and a reasonable, well-framed counter almost never costs you the offer. What damages your position is negotiating badly: no research behind the number, or framing it as an ultimatum instead of a conversation.

## Before You Say a Number
- Know the market range for your role, experience level, and city — Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, and AmbitionBox give rough bands; cross-check at least two sources since any single one can be skewed
- Know your own number — the minimum you'd accept, decided before the call, not during it
- Never give a number first if you can avoid it — if asked for your expectation early in the process, it's fine to say "I'd like to understand the role better before discussing numbers" once or twice, though most companies will eventually ask directly and you should have a range ready

## The Actual Negotiation

### When they give you a number
Don't accept or reject immediately, even if it's good. Say: "Thank you, let me review this and get back to you." A day of space costs you nothing and signals you're evaluating seriously, not just grateful for any offer.

### When you counter
Anchor slightly above what you want, since the number often moves down slightly during discussion. Frame it around market data and your specific value, not personal need: "Based on my research for this role and experience level, I was expecting closer to ₹X — is there flexibility here?" is stronger than "I need more because of my expenses."

### If they say the number is fixed
Salary isn't the only lever. Joining bonus, number of paid leave days, remote work flexibility, a signing stock grant, or an accelerated first review (6 months instead of 12) are all negotiable even when base salary isn't.

## A Real Script
This is close to the actual wording I used: "Thank you for the offer — I'm excited about the role. Based on my research on market rates for [role] with [X years] of experience in [city], and the scope of what we discussed, I was hoping for something closer to ₹[number]. Is there room to move on that?"
That's it. No apology, no over-explaining, no ultimatum. State the ask, give the reasoning, wait for their response.

## Mistakes That Cost People Money
- Negotiating before you have a written offer — verbal numbers shift; wait for the formal offer letter before pushing
- Comparing yourself to a friend's package without accounting for company size, role, or city — it weakens your case instead of strengthening it
- Accepting on the spot out of relief or fear of losing the offer — a reasonable ask for 24-48 hours to review is standard and expected
- Framing it emotionally ("I really need this") instead of around market value and your specific contribution

## What If They Say No
A firm "no" on salary doesn't mean the conversation is over — it means you shift to the other levers above. And if a company reacts badly to a single reasonable, well-researched counter-offer, that's information too: it's a preview of how they'll handle every future compensation conversation once you're employed there.

## Negotiating as a Fresher With No Leverage
Freshers often assume they have no room to negotiate since they have no competing offers and limited experience to point to. There's still room — even a modest, well-researched ask ("I noticed the range for this role in this city is typically X-Y, could we look at the higher end given my project experience") costs nothing to raise and is a normal part of the process even for entry-level roles. The worst realistic outcome is a polite no, not a withdrawn offer.

## Negotiating a Counter-Offer From Your Current Employer
If a competing offer prompts your current employer to counter, treat the situation carefully — accepting a counter-offer to stay is statistically associated with leaving within a year anyway, often because the underlying reasons for looking elsewhere (growth, recognition, management) rarely get fixed by a raise alone. If you do consider a counter-offer, ask what changes beyond the number — role, growth path, reporting line — not just the salary figure.

## Reading the Room During the Conversation
A recruiter who immediately says "let me check" and comes back with a better number quickly is signaling there was room built into the original offer from the start. A recruiter who pushes back hard on a modest, well-justified ask is telling you something about the budget or the company's flexibility more broadly — information worth factoring into whether this is the right offer regardless of the final number.

## Should You Disclose Your Current Salary
You're not obligated to, and in several Indian states this question is increasingly discouraged anyway. A neutral line like "I'd prefer to focus on the market rate for this role and the value I'd bring" sidesteps it without sounding evasive. On how much to ask for above the offer, 10 to 20% above the initial number, grounded in whatever market research you did beforehand, is usually reasonable. Asking for 50% more with no clear justification tends to read as unrealistic rather than confident.

## Why Silence After You Counter Isn't a Bad Sign
A day or two of no response after sending your counter usually means the recruiter is checking internally, not reconsidering the offer entirely. Resist the urge to follow up within hours out of anxiety — a reasonable gap between your ask and their answer is completely normal for anything that needs sign-off beyond the person you're talking to.

## Negotiating Non-Salary Compensation in Detail
Joining bonuses are often easier to move than base salary, since they're a one-time cost rather than a recurring one on the company's books — worth asking about if base salary has no flexibility. Similarly, an accelerated performance review (at 6 months instead of the standard 12) costs the company nothing upfront but gives you a faster path to a raise if you perform well, and it's a request companies grant more often than people expect simply because so few candidates think to ask for it.

## Timing the Negotiation Within the Hiring Process
Negotiate after the offer is extended, not during early interview rounds — raising compensation too early can read as prioritizing pay over fit, while raising it after an offer is standard practice and expected by any experienced recruiter. The one exception is if you're asked directly for your expectation early on; in that case, a wide, honest range beats deflecting entirely.

## What to Do If the Recruiter Asks for a Number in the First Call
Being pushed for a number before you've done any research is a real risk, a wide, honest range based on general market awareness, "somewhere in the range of X to Y depending on the full scope," buys time to research properly before the formal offer stage without appearing evasive or unprepared for the question, which is a more comfortable position than being locked into a number you gave without any real research behind it.

## Why Written Confirmation of Any Verbal Promise Matters
Any additional benefit negotiated beyond the base number, an accelerated review, a signing bonus, a specific joining date, should appear in the written offer letter itself, not just a verbal assurance during a call, since verbal promises have a way of not making it into HR's actual records once the hiring process moves on to onboarding and the person who made the promise may not even still be involved by then.

## Practicing the Actual Conversation Before You Have It
Saying the negotiation script out loud once or twice beforehand, even alone in a room, makes a genuine difference in how confident and natural it sounds during the real conversation, since reading words silently in your head and speaking them under mild pressure are very different experiences that reward a small amount of deliberate rehearsal.

## What Happens If You Ask and They Say Yes Immediately
An instant yes to your first number sometimes means you asked for less than they were prepared to offer. It's not a reason for regret — you still moved the number up from where it started. Note it for next time: if the answer comes back too fast and too easy, the anchor you set might have room to go a little higher, and that's useful information to carry into the next negotiation, not something to second-guess about the one you already closed and accepted.

## Ask Anyway
Treat it as one more conversation in a process you're already having, not a confrontation. Grounded research and the willingness to ask move the number more than any clever phrasing — most offers never get a counter at all, so the number on the table becomes final by default. Don't let yours be one of them. The number rarely moves for people who never bring it up in the first place.
