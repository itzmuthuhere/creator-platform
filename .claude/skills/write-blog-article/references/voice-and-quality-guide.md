# Voice and quality guide

Techpulzo.in (this site) was rejected once by Google AdSense for "low value content." The fix that worked wasn't just adding more articles — it was removing thin ones and rewriting the rest so the site didn't read as a single voice churning out a template. This file has the concrete detail behind that fix. Read it before drafting, not after.

## The two authors

Every post needs a real, consistent first-person voice matching one of these two people. Don't invent a third.

- **Muthu** — Java developer. Writes about tech, career growth, and personal finance from the perspective of someone actually working in the field. Natural first-person material: things seen on the job, mistakes made personally, teammates' experiences, actual numbers from his own life (a phone, a loan, a negotiation).
- **Nithiyaraj** — influencer. Writes about everyday tech and lifestyle. Lighter, more casual register than Muthu.

Pick whichever fits the topic and commit to it — a tech/tutorial/career/finance piece is almost always Muthu.

## Why "aggregator" content gets flagged, and what to write instead

The single biggest reason strong-sounding articles got removed this round: they were secretly just lists of well-known things with no original angle. "10 Best Free Websites to Learn Coding," "Best Free AI Tools," and "What is Artificial Intelligence" all got pulled for this. Each one restates information anyone could get from the products' own homepages — no firsthand testing, no unique framework, no opinion that couldn't have been generated by skimming five other blog posts on the same topic.

**What survived and what got written since instead** has one thing in common: it explains a *mechanism*, does *real math*, or takes a *specific stance*, not just "here are N things."

- "Why Your Wi-Fi Feels Slow in Certain Rooms" — actual RF physics (2.4GHz vs 5GHz trade-offs, why brick/metal/water attenuate signal), not "5 tips to boost your wifi"
- "How Database Indexes Actually Work" — the B-tree mechanism, a worked before/after, `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` as a real diagnostic step
- "Is an MBA Actually Worth It in India" — a genuine break-even calculation with foregone salary counted in, not a generic pros/cons list
- "How Much Home Loan You Can Actually Afford" — the gap between what a bank approves (FOIR) and what's actually comfortable, with the hidden first-year costs most people miss

Before drafting, ask: **could this article have been written by someone who just skimmed five competitors' posts on the same topic, with no personal stake or deeper mechanism to explain?** If yes, it's an aggregator piece — change the angle or don't write it. A "best tools" or "top N" framing is a strong signal to stop and reconsider, not a checklist to fill in.

## The corpus-level trap: sounding fine alone, sounding templated together

Per-article quality isn't the whole story. Read across several articles at once and a pattern becomes obvious even when each one is individually well-written. This is what actually got flagged in the audit that led to this skill:

- **9 of 13 self-written articles ended with the literal identical heading "The Real Takeaway."** Vary the closing heading every time, and tailor it to that specific article: "Ask Anyway," "Two Habits, Not Tricks," "Compare Totals, Not Monthlies," "Trust Your Own Number, Not the Bank's Ceiling." Never reuse a closing heading across articles.
- **Every article opened with the same rhetorical move**: a cold-open scenario or number, then a contrastive pivot sentence. Vary this deliberately — sometimes a direct claim, sometimes a quote, sometimes a plain stat comparison, sometimes a question, sometimes just starting mid-thought.
- **"Actually" and "genuinely" were wildly overused** — 77 and 27 occurrences respectively across 24 articles, and 11 of 24 *titles* contained "actually." Use these words sparingly — 0-2 per article is plenty. If you notice yourself reaching for "actually" to add emphasis, cut it; the sentence almost always works without it.
- **The "X isn't Y — it's Z" contrastive construction was a crutch used everywhere.** ("Your battery isn't broken — it's chemically consumed." "This isn't a DNS problem — it's Google's cache.") It's a fine sentence once. It's a tell when every third paragraph does it. Vary sentence rhythm instead.
- **Em-dashes were dense and uniform across every single article.** Not banned, just watch the density — mix in commas, periods, and semicolons so paragraphs don't all have the same cadence.

Run `scripts/check-content.js` against the draft before publishing — it flags word overuse, the contrastive pattern, and em-dash counts automatically. But the tool can only catch what's mechanically countable; read the piece next to one or two other recent articles and ask whether it sounds like the same person wrote all of them in the same fifteen minutes.

## Weaving in first-person voice — vary placement and material

Every article should have a real personal touch near the opening and another near the close (sometimes mid-article instead — don't make this rigid). What varies from article to article:

- **Where**: sometimes the opening line, sometimes buried in the middle of a section, sometimes only in the conclusion
- **What kind of claim**: a mistake made personally, a friend or colleague's story, a number checked on your own device/account, something noticed while screening resumes or reviewing code at work, a test run at home
- **Register**: sometimes confident and specific ("I ran these numbers in my own apartment out of frustration"), sometimes a small confession ("I still remember how rough my own first project looked")

Concrete examples actually used:
- "I helped a cousin pick a phone in this exact price range last month, and it reminded me how much this segment has changed."
- "I've screened resumes for open roles on my own team, and it really is that fast."
- "My own phone is two years old right now, so this one's personal."
- "A colleague asked me to help her think through this decision last year."

Don't write a template for this — if every article's personal touch reads as "I [did the exact same kind of thing] once," that's the same corpus-level trap described above, just moved to a new sentence.

## No orphan sections

FAQ content belongs in the post's structured `faqItems` field (rendered separately on the page by its own component), never as inline `<h2>Questions People Ask</h2>` followed by nothing. This exact bug — an empty heading immediately followed by the next heading — showed up repeatedly from leftover outline placeholders. `check-content.js` flags near-empty sections automatically; if it fires, either delete the heading or the content genuinely wasn't finished.

## Staying current

Don't cite a specific product version, model name, or price that will look dated in a year (a real example caught in this session: an article referencing "GPT-3.5" long after that model was retired). General claims about capability age better than pinned specifics. If you must reference something time-sensitive, frame it as "as of [year]" rather than stating it as a permanent fact.

## Length and structure

- Target 600-900+ words of real body content. Thin articles (under ~500 words) were part of what triggered the original rejection.
- Use real `<h2>`/`<h3>` structure, `<table>` for comparisons, `<ul>`/`<ol>` for genuine lists, `<code>`/`<pre><code>` for anything code-related. Don't force a table or list where prose reads better — vary the shape article to article the same way you vary voice.
