# What paying only the credit card minimum due costs you

Every credit card statement in India carries a line most people skim past. RBI requires card issuers to print a warning that paying only the minimum "would result in the repayment stretching over months / years with consequential compounded interest payment." That line is about the credit card minimum due, and it understates the problem. Paying the minimum on ₹1 lakh can take 16 to 22 years. Paying *most* of a bill but not all of it is also far more expensive than it looks.

Here is how the minimum is worked out, what it protects you from, what it costs, and how to get out if you're already carrying a balance.

## What the minimum amount due is

Your statement shows two numbers. The **total amount due** is everything you owe. The **minimum amount due** (MAD) is the smallest payment that keeps your account from being treated as overdue. Pay it by the due date and the bank won't charge a late fee or report you as late. Everything you didn't pay rolls into next month, with interest.

The formula is set by each card issuer, not by RBI. RBI's [credit card directions](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=12300) only require that paying the minimum never lets the debt grow (no "negative amortisation"), and that unpaid fees and taxes aren't themselves charged interest. Two large issuers currently use quite different formulas:

| | HDFC Bank (from June 2025) | SBI Card (from 15 July 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Always included in full | GST, EMIs due, fees and charges, over-limit amount | GST, EMIs due, fees and charges, finance charges (interest), over-limit amount |
| Plus | 5% of (interest + purchases + cash advances), or all the interest plus 5% of purchases and cash, whichever is higher | 2% of the remaining balance |

*Sources: [HDFC Bank MITC](https://www.hdfc.bank.in/content/dam/hdfcbankpws/in/en/personal-banking/discover-products/cards/credit-cards/personal-mitc/mitc-in-english.pdf), version dated September 2026; [SBI Card MITC](https://www.sbicard.com/sbi-card-en/assets/docs/pdf/most-important-terms-and-conditions/mitc-english.pdf).*

Either way, the minimum is built to cover the interest and a small slice of what you borrowed. That slice is the whole problem.

## What paying only the minimum costs

Both HDFC Bank and SBI Card charge up to 3.75% a month on a revolving balance, which is 45% a year. GST at 18% is added on top of the interest, so the real cost is about 53% a year. On a ₹1 lakh balance, the first month's interest is ₹3,750 plus ₹675 of GST: ₹4,425 before a rupee of the debt goes down.

Suppose you owe ₹1 lakh, stop using the card, and pay exactly the minimum on time every month. Simulating both formulas:

| How you pay | Time to clear ₹1 lakh | Interest + GST paid | Total paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum only, 5% formula | 22.2 years | ₹3.0 lakh | ₹4.0 lakh |
| Minimum only, SBI Card's 2% formula | 16.6 years | ₹2.2 lakh | ₹3.2 lakh |
| Twice the minimum, 5% formula | 5.4 years | ₹60,000 | ₹1.6 lakh |
| A fixed ₹5,000 a month | 4.2 years | ₹1.5 lakh | ₹2.5 lakh |
| A fixed ₹10,000 a month | 1.2 years | ₹35,000 | ₹1.35 lakh |

*Assumes 3.75% a month plus 18% GST, no new spending on the card, and a ₹200 minimum payment once the balance gets small (our assumption, so the last rupees get cleared). Our calculation.*

On the 5% formula, each payment covers that month's interest and GST with only about 1.4% of the balance left over for the debt itself. The balance shrinks so slowly that you pay for the original ₹1 lakh about four times over.

SBI Card's newer formula is less bad than it sounds. "2%" looks smaller than "5%", but because it also takes 100% of the interest and GST, about 2% of the debt is repaid each month instead of 1.4%. That's why it clears faster: it pushes minimum-only payers to repay more each month.

The minimum is also a psychological trap. A 2009 study by psychologist Neil Stewart, [published in Psychological Science](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/2552/), found that printing a minimum payment on a statement has an unintended effect: the number acts as an anchor that pulls repayments down. The number on your statement is a floor, but it reads like a suggestion.

## Paying most of the bill is expensive too

This is the part most explainers miss. If you pay anything less than the full amount, even ₹1 less, you lose the interest-free period on that bill, and interest is charged **from the date of each purchase on the whole amount**, not just on what you left unpaid.

HDFC's MITC states it plainly: if you pay less than the total due, finance charges apply "on all transactions from the date of transaction", and every new purchase also attracts interest "till such time as the previous outstanding amounts are repaid in full". SBI Card's terms charge interest from "the effective date of the transaction" in the same situation. RBI's directions require issuers to explain that the interest-free period is suspended while any previous balance is outstanding.

Here is what that means with numbers. Your bill is ₹50,000, made up of purchases from about 20 days before the statement date. The due date is 20 days after the statement. You pay ₹45,000 on the due date and leave ₹5,000:

- Interest on the full ₹50,000 for about 40 days, from purchase to payment: about ₹2,470
- Interest on the remaining ₹5,000 until the next statement: about ₹60
- GST on that interest: about ₹455

**Total: about ₹3,000 on your next statement**, for leaving ₹5,000 unpaid for a month. That's 60% of the unpaid amount, in one cycle.

It continues while the balance stays. If you keep using the card for, say, ₹20,000 a month of normal spending, that spending gets no interest-free days either: roughly another ₹580 a month in interest and GST. People who carry a small balance "just this once" while still using the card for everything are often the ones who get hit hardest.

## What paying the minimum does protect

The minimum isn't worthless. Paying it on time:

- **Avoids the late fee.** SBI Card's fee runs from ₹100 to ₹1,300 depending on the amount outstanding, plus ₹100 more if you miss the minimum two cycles in a row. RBI only allows the late fee on the amount outstanding after the due date, not on the whole bill.
- **Keeps you off the "late" list.** Under RBI's rules, a card account can be reported as past due to credit bureaus, or charged penal fees, only once it is more than three days past due. Paying the minimum keeps the account current.
- **Prevents default.** SBI Card's MITC treats an account as a non-performing asset if the minimum isn't fully paid within 90 days of the due date, and a single NPA card makes every SBI card you hold NPA.

So if you can't pay in full this month, always pay at least the minimum. Just don't treat it as a repayment plan.

A large unpaid balance can still hurt your [credit score](https://techpulzo.in/credit-score-in-india-how-it-works-and-how-to-fix-it) even while you pay the minimum, because it pushes up how much of your credit limit you're using.

## How to get out of a revolving balance

If you're already carrying a balance, the order of moves matters.

1. **Stop putting new spending on that card.** Until the balance is cleared, every purchase is charged interest from day one. Use a debit card or UPI for daily spending meanwhile.
2. **Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum.** Pick the largest amount you can sustain and set it as a standing instruction. On ₹1 lakh, a fixed ₹10,000 a month clears it in about 14 months; the minimum takes 16 to 22 years.
3. **Move the balance to a cheaper rate.** Most issuers let you convert an outstanding balance into an EMI loan. HDFC Bank's [SmartEMI](https://www.hdfc.bank.in/smartemi) runs for 6 to 48 months on transactions up to 60 days old. The rates it published for April–June 2026 averaged 18.2% a year (an APR of 21.57%), far less than 53%. Check the processing fee before you convert. A personal loan from your bank is another option. In Zee Business's interview on the topic, financial planner Harshvardhan Roongta also mentions balance transfers to another card at a lower rate for a few months. Compare any of these on the APR, which RBI requires issuers to disclose, not on the monthly EMI.
4. **Use savings if you have them.** An investment earning 7–12% a year makes no sense while you pay 53% on a card. Breaking a fixed deposit to clear the balance is usually the best return you'll get all year. Keep enough for a genuine emergency, then pay.
5. **Don't let it become a cycle.** Roongta's point is worth repeating: a loan to clear a card is a one-time rescue. If the card balance builds up again, the problem is the spending, and a cheaper loan only buys time.

If your income drops so far that you can't pay even the minimum, call the issuer before the due date and ask what restructuring or EMI options exist. A missed minimum leads to late fees, bureau reporting after three days, and NPA status after 90.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if I pay only the minimum amount due?

You avoid the late fee and your account isn't reported as late, but everything you didn't pay is charged interest, about 3.75% a month plus GST at most issuers. You also lose the interest-free period: the unpaid bill is charged interest from each purchase date, and new purchases are charged from the day you make them until the balance is fully cleared.

### Is it better to pay the minimum or nothing at all?

Always pay at least the minimum. Paying nothing adds a late fee (up to ₹1,300 at SBI Card), can get your account reported as past due once it's more than three days late, and moves you towards default. The interest on the unpaid amount is charged either way, so the minimum saves you money and protects your credit history.

### Does paying the minimum due affect my credit score?

Not directly. Paying the minimum on time keeps the account current, so it isn't reported as late. But a large balance carried month to month raises your credit utilisation, the share of your limit in use, which lenders and bureaus watch. A high utilisation can lower your score even when every payment is on time.

### If I pay the full amount next month, do I get my interest-free period back?

Yes, once the balance is cleared in full, new purchases get the normal interest-free days again. But the bill you clear will still include interest charged on the earlier balance and on purchases made while you were carrying it. The interest-free period returns only for spending after the slate is clean.

### Why did my minimum amount due go up?

Usually because the issuer changed its formula or because your bill now includes EMIs, fees or interest that are included in full. SBI Card raised its minimum for many cardholders from 15 July 2025 by including 100% of interest and EMIs; HDFC Bank revised its formula from June 2025. Your card's MITC on the issuer's website shows the current formula.

## The minimum is a floor

Treat the minimum due as the line between "expensive" and "damaging": below it you get late fees and a mark on your credit report, at it you get 53% a year. For your own card, check whether your last statement was paid in full. If it wasn't, the interest-free period is already gone, so the cheapest fix is to clear the whole balance, or move it to an EMI at a third of the rate, before you swipe again.
