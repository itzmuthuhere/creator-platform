# Health insurance room rent limits, co-pay and sub-limits, explained

When the blogger behind freefincal claimed for his mother's surgery in 2018, the room cost ₹500 a day more than the limit the insurer applied. The insurer then cut the surgeon's fee, the consultation fee and the operation theatre charges by the same proportion, and the family paid about ₹24,000 that the policy was meant to cover. He showed the settlement sheet line by line in a video about it.

That's a health insurance room rent limit at work, and it's one of three clauses that decide how much of a hospital bill your policy pays. The other two are co-payment and sub-limits. All three are legal and common, and all three are printed in your policy, usually far from the sum insured on the first page. Here's how each works under the current rules, with a worked claim.

## The three clauses at a glance

| Clause | What it means | Example from IRDAI's standard policy |
|---|---|---|
| Room-rent limit | The most the policy pays per day for the room. Choose a costlier room and some related charges are cut in proportion. | Up to 2% of the sum insured, maximum ₹5,000 a day. ICU up to 5%, maximum ₹10,000. |
| Co-payment | A fixed share of every admissible claim that you pay yourself. | 5% of every claim. |
| Sub-limit | A cap on a specific treatment, below your sum insured. | Cataract: 25% of the sum insured or ₹40,000, whichever is lower. Certain modern treatments: 50%. |

*The examples come from Arogya Sanjeevani, IRDAI's standard health policy sold by many insurers, as listed in [IRDAI's FAQ](https://irdai.gov.in/faq-s-on-standard-health-product-arogya-sanjeevani-product) and [New India Assurance's version](https://www.newindia.co.in/health-insurance/arogya-sanjeevani-policy).*

Arogya Sanjeevani is a useful reference precisely because it has all three. Many other policies have some, none, or different versions of them.

## Room-rent limits and proportionate deduction

A room-rent limit can be written three ways: a fixed amount per day, a percentage of the sum insured, or a room category such as "single private room". If you stay within it, the room is covered in full.

If you take a costlier room, the insurer doesn't just refuse the extra rent. Hospitals usually charge more for doctors' visits, surgery, nursing and the operation theatre when you're in a more expensive room. So the insurer pays those **room-linked charges** in the same proportion as your eligible room rent bears to the room you actually took. That's called proportionate deduction.

### What can't be cut

Several explainers, including videos by Ditto and Niva Bupa, describe the deduction as applying to the whole bill. IRDAI's [June 2020 norms on proportionate deduction](https://irdai.gov.in/documents/37343/365525/Modified+Guidelines+on+Product+filing+in+Health+Insurance+Business-Norms+on.pdf/7b50effc-e553-f0a6-389d-3ca90ab11af5?version=1.0&t=1631531502137&download=true) narrowed it. Insurers must define which "associated medical expenses" can be cut, and these can never be included:

- medicines and consumables;
- implants and medical devices;
- diagnostic tests.

The norms also bar proportionate deduction **on ICU charges** altogether, and at **hospitals that don't charge differently by room category**, or for any charge that doesn't vary by room. They apply to products filed from October 2020, and existing policies had to comply at renewal from April 2021.

freefincal's 2018 settlement already followed this pattern: the medicines were paid in full, while the surgeon, consultation and theatre fees were cut.

### A worked claim

Say you have a ₹5 lakh policy on Arogya Sanjeevani terms: room rent up to ₹5,000 a day and a 5% co-pay. You take an ₹8,000 room for four days. Your eligible share is ₹5,000 ÷ ₹8,000 = 62.5%.

| Bill item | Billed | What the insurer counts |
|---|---|---|
| Room (₹8,000 × 4 days) | ₹32,000 | ₹20,000 (62.5%) |
| Doctor, surgeon, theatre and nursing (room-linked) | ₹90,000 | ₹56,250 (62.5%) |
| Medicines and consumables | ₹45,000 | ₹45,000 (can't be cut) |
| Diagnostics | ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 (can't be cut) |
| Non-medical items (registration, toiletries etc.) | ₹3,000 | ₹0 |
| **Total** | **₹1,90,000** | **₹1,41,250** |

The 5% co-pay is then taken from the admissible ₹1,41,250: ₹7,062. **The insurer pays ₹1,34,188 and you pay ₹55,812.**

*Illustrative bill. Our calculation.*

Two comparisons put that in perspective:

- **Under the old "whole bill" approach**, the insurer would have counted only 62.5% of everything payable and paid about ₹1.11 lakh. You'd have paid about ₹79,000. The 2020 rules save you roughly ₹23,000 on this bill.
- **In an eligible ₹5,000 room**, assuming the hospital charges proportionately less for room-linked services, the bill would be about ₹1.44 lakh. The insurer would pay the same ₹1.34 lakh, and you'd pay about ₹10,000, mostly the co-pay.

So under today's rules, upgrading the room costs you roughly what the hospital charges extra for it: the higher rent and the higher room-linked fees, about ₹45,000 here. It's a real cost, but a predictable one, and you can decide at admission whether it's worth it.

Many newer policies drop room-rent limits altogether, as Ditto's video points out. If you're buying a new policy, compare the premium with and without one; for many families the difference is worth paying.

## Co-payment

A co-pay is a fixed percentage of every admissible claim that you pay, however small or large the claim. In financial planner Gurleen Kaur Tikku's explainer, it shows up in three forms:

- **Mandatory co-pay**, often in senior-citizen policies or from a set age such as 60 or 61. You can't remove it.
- **Voluntary co-pay**, which you choose in exchange for a lower premium.
- **Zone-based co-pay**, which applies if you're treated in a costlier city than the zone your premium was priced for.

Two details from the standard policy wording are worth knowing. The co-pay is applied to the **admissible** amount, after room-rent adjustments and sub-limits, as in the worked claim above. And a co-payment [does not reduce your sum insured](https://www.eindiainsurance.com/brochure/cholamandalam-health-arogya-sanjeevani-policy-wording.pdf): you pay your share, but the full amount the insurer paid is what's deducted from your cover.

A 5% co-pay on a ₹2 lakh claim is ₹10,000; 20% would be ₹40,000. Voluntary co-pay only makes sense if you could comfortably pay that share of a large claim out of savings.

## Sub-limits

A sub-limit caps what the policy pays for a particular treatment or item, regardless of your sum insured. Common ones are cataract surgery, knee replacement and certain advanced treatments, or limits on things like ambulance charges.

Arogya Sanjeevani caps cataract at 25% of the sum insured or ₹40,000, whichever is lower. With the 5% co-pay:

| Cataract surgery cost | Insurer pays | You pay |
|---|---|---|
| ₹35,000 | ₹33,250 | ₹1,750 |
| ₹65,000 (premium lens) | ₹38,000 | ₹27,000 |

*Our calculation.*

Disease-wise sub-limits tend to appear in older or lower-priced policies, and they matter most on parents' policies, since cataract and joint replacements are common later in life.

## How to check your own policy

1. **Open the Customer Information Sheet (CIS).** IRDAI's [2024 health master circular](https://irdai.gov.in/documents/37343/365525/%e0%a4%b8%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%b5%e0%a4%be%e0%a4%b8%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%a5%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%af+%e0%a4%ac%e0%a5%80%e0%a4%ae%e0%a4%be+%e0%a4%b5%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%af%e0%a4%b5%e0%a4%b8%e0%a4%be%e0%a4%af+%e0%a4%aa%e0%a4%b0+%e0%a4%ae%e0%a4%be%e0%a4%b8%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%9f%e0%a4%b0+%e0%a4%aa%e0%a4%b0%e0%a4%bf%e0%a4%aa%e0%a4%a4%e0%a5%8d%e0%a4%b0+_+Master+Circular++on+Health++Insurance+Business++29052024.pdf/5e707a91-b5de-1ec1-cf18-b66273a6839d?t=1716962621002&version=1.0) requires every policy to come with this one-page summary, and it must list sub-limits, deductibles and waiting periods. The room-rent limit and co-pay are usually under "loss sharing" or "cost sharing".
2. **Note the room limit in rupees.** If it's a percentage, multiply it out: 1% of ₹3 lakh is ₹3,000 a day. Compare that with what hospitals near you charge for a room you'd accept.
3. **Check for an age-triggered co-pay**, especially on a parent's policy.
4. **At admission, ask the hospital** which room category your policy covers and whether its doctor and procedure charges vary by room. The cashless desk can usually tell you.
5. **Check the settlement sheet.** If you see deductions on medicines, consumables, implants, diagnostics or ICU charges marked as "proportionate", question them with the insurer under IRDAI's 2020 norms, and escalate to the insurer's grievance cell or the Insurance Ombudsman if needed.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is proportionate deduction in health insurance?

When you take a room costlier than your policy allows, the insurer pays room-linked charges, such as doctor visits, surgery and nursing, in the same ratio as your eligible room rent to the actual rent. Since IRDAI's 2020 norms, it can't be applied to medicines, consumables, implants, diagnostics or ICU charges, or at hospitals that don't vary their charges by room type.

### Does the room rent limit apply to ICU?

Under IRDAI's 2020 norms, insurers can't apply proportionate deduction to ICU charges, because hospitals don't have different categories of ICU. Your policy may still cap the ICU daily charge itself, as Arogya Sanjeevani does at 5% of the sum insured or ₹10,000 a day. If the ICU costs more than that cap, you pay the difference, but other bill items aren't cut because of it.

### Is a policy with no room rent limit worth the extra premium?

Often, yes. Check what a room you'd accept costs at hospitals near you. If a limit like 1% of a ₹3–5 lakh sum insured falls short of that, you'd pay the upgrade's room-linked charges every time you're admitted. If you live where hospital rooms are cheap and you're comfortable with a shared room, a capped policy can be adequate.

### Does co-pay reduce my sum insured?

No, not the part you pay. Under standard policy wordings, the co-pay is your share of the admissible claim; only the amount the insurer actually pays is deducted from your sum insured. A ₹1 lakh admissible claim with 5% co-pay uses ₹95,000 of your cover, and you pay ₹5,000.

### Can I remove a co-pay from my existing policy?

It depends on the type. Ask your insurer whether a voluntary co-pay can be dropped at renewal for a higher premium. A mandatory co-pay, such as one that starts at a certain age, is part of the product, so avoiding it usually means moving to a different policy. If you consider porting, check how much waiting-period credit carries over before you switch.

## Read the cost-sharing page before the hospital does

Your sum insured is the headline, but the cost-sharing page decides the cheque. Find your room limit in rupees, any co-pay and its trigger age, and the sub-limits on treatments your family is likely to need. Then decide, at a calm moment rather than at the admission desk, whether to upgrade the policy or accept those limits. The same habit of reading past the headline number pays off with loans too, whether it's [what buy-now-pay-later fine print hides](https://techpulzo.in/the-real-cost-of-buy-now-pay-later-what-the-fine-print-does-not-say) or [a loan's APR instead of its EMI](https://techpulzo.in/how-to-read-a-loan-apr-instead-of-just-the-emi).
