# Home loan balance transfer: when switching lenders pays off, and when it doesn't

A home loan balance transfer moves your outstanding loan to a new lender at a lower interest rate. Done at the right time it can save lakhs. Done carelessly it costs money: the fees eat the early savings, and a quietly longer tenure can leave you paying more interest than before.

This guide shows how to work out whether a switch pays for you, with numbers, what it costs, the cheaper option most people skip (asking your own bank), and the steps if you go ahead.

## The quick test

A balance transfer usually makes sense when all three are true:

1. **Your rate is meaningfully higher** than what the new lender offers you, roughly 0.5 percentage points or more.
2. **Plenty of loan is left**, usually more than seven or eight years and a large outstanding balance. Paisabazaar's explainer for [The Hindu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YuWlLUTA5h8) points out that early in a loan most of each EMI is interest, so that's when a lower rate saves most.
3. **Your own lender won't match the rate** for a small fee.

If your loan is on an older benchmark (base rate or MCLR), the same video notes you may also be missing rate cuts that newer repo-linked loans pass on faster, which is another reason to look.

## What a 1% lower rate is worth

Take a ₹40 lakh balance with 15 years left at 8.9%, moved to 7.9%. The EMI on the old loan is ₹40,333.

| After the switch | EMI | Interest still to pay | Compared with staying |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stay at 8.9% | ₹40,333 | ₹32.6 lakh | — |
| Same 15 years at 7.9% | ₹37,996 | ₹28.4 lakh | **₹4.2 lakh less** |
| Keep paying ₹40,333 at 7.9% | ₹40,333 (ends ~19 months early) | ₹25.0 lakh | **₹7.6 lakh less** |
| New lender stretches it to 25 years | ₹30,608 | ₹51.8 lakh | **₹19.2 lakh more** |

*Calculated with the standard EMI formula; figures rounded.*

The last row is the trap. A lower EMI feels like a saving, but adding ten years more than wipes out the lower rate. Real Indian Money's [walkthrough](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SjfX5kL2uKs) shows the same thing with its own numbers: borrowers who accept the new bank's suggested EMI or tenure can end up paying more interest after the switch. **Ask the new lender to keep your remaining tenure, or to keep your current EMI.**

## What switching costs

There are three main costs, and they vary by lender and state:

- **Processing fee** at the new lender, often 0.5–1% of the loan plus GST, sometimes waived in offers.
- **Legal and valuation charges** for the new lender's checks on the property.
- **Stamp duty on the new mortgage** (the memorandum of deposit of title deeds), which varies by state.

[Moneyview's list](https://moneyview.in/home-loan/home-loan-transfer-charges) also includes CERSAI registration and small admin fees. For the ₹40 lakh example, a 0.5% fee plus GST (₹23,600), 0.2% stamp duty (₹8,000) and ₹10,000 of legal and valuation charges come to about **₹41,600**. At ₹2,338 a month lower EMI, that's recovered in about **18 months**.

**No foreclosure penalty on a floating-rate loan.** Banks have been barred from charging foreclosure or pre-payment penalties on floating-rate term loans to individuals since an [RBI circular of May 2014](https://www.rbi.org.in/commonman/english/Scripts/Notification.aspx?Id=1381), and housing finance companies face similar limits. The RBI's Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025 extend this across banks, NBFCs and other regulated lenders for loans sanctioned or renewed from 2026, with no lock-in period, [News On AIR reported](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/rbi-bars-pre-payment-charges-on-transfer-of-floating-rate-loans-for-individual-borrowers/). Fixed-rate loans can still carry a charge, so check your agreement.

## Try your own bank first

Many lenders let existing borrowers move to their current, lower rate for a one-time fee, with no new paperwork or stamp duty. [HDFC Bank's conversion fee](https://homeloans.hdfc.bank.in/conversion-fees) for switching a fully disbursed variable-rate home loan to a lower rate is 0.25% of the principal outstanding or ₹5,000 plus taxes, whichever is lower. On a ₹40 lakh loan that's about ₹5,900, against roughly ₹41,600 for a full transfer.

Write to your bank asking for the rate offered to new borrowers, and mention you're considering a transfer. If they match it, you've saved the switching costs and the hassle.

## When a transfer isn't worth it

- **Little time left.** In the example, after 10 years only about ₹19.5 lakh is outstanding with five years to go. The same 1% cut then saves about ₹56,000 in total, barely more than the switching costs.
- **A small rate gap.** Below about 0.25–0.5 percentage points, fees take years to recover.
- **Rates are about to move.** The RBI's repo rate has been 5.25% since late 2025, and [some analysts expect](https://www.gopocket.in/news/rbi-policy-october-2026-repo-rate-inflation-emi-outlook) pressure to raise it as inflation picked up to 4.82% in August 2026. Repo-linked loans at both lenders move with it, so compare the **spread** over the benchmark, which is what actually stays different.
- **Your credit score has fallen.** The new lender prices on your current profile; a weaker score may mean no better rate. Our guide to [credit scores](https://techpulzo.in/credit-score-in-india-how-it-works-and-how-to-fix-it) explains what lenders look at.

## How to do a balance transfer

1. **Get your numbers.** Ask your current lender for an outstanding balance letter and the list of property documents they hold.
2. **Ask your own bank to match** the new rate (see above).
3. **Apply to the new lender** with your ID, income proof, the balance letter, the document list and property papers. They'll do legal and valuation checks and issue a sanction letter. Check the **rate, spread, tenure and every fee** in it.
4. **Request foreclosure** from the old lender. The new lender pays them directly.
5. **Collect your original documents** and a no-dues certificate from the old lender, and make sure the new lender registers its charge.
6. **Set up the new EMI** and cancel the old auto-debit once the old loan shows closed.

The steps follow Moneyview's guide; the timeline is usually a few weeks, mostly for the legal checks.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should I transfer my home loan to another bank?

When the new rate is at least about 0.5 percentage points lower, you still have many years and a large balance left, and your own bank won't lower your rate for a small conversion fee. Check that the savings recover the switching costs within a year or two.

### Is there a foreclosure charge on a home loan balance transfer?

Not on a floating-rate home loan taken by an individual. Banks have been barred from such charges since 2014, and the RBI's 2025 directions extend the rule to all regulated lenders for loans sanctioned or renewed from 2026. Fixed-rate loans can still carry a charge under the loan agreement.

### What are the charges for a home loan balance transfer?

Mainly the new lender's processing fee (often 0.5–1% plus GST), legal and valuation fees, and state stamp duty on the new mortgage, plus small items such as CERSAI registration. On a ₹40 lakh loan these can add up to around ₹40,000 or more.

### Does a balance transfer reduce my EMI or my tenure?

Either, depending on what you agree with the new lender. Keeping your current EMI at the lower rate saves the most interest. Accepting a longer tenure to cut the EMI can make the loan more expensive overall.

### Can I ask my existing bank for a lower rate instead?

Yes. Many banks let existing borrowers switch to a lower rate for a conversion fee. HDFC Bank, for example, charges 0.25% of the outstanding or ₹5,000 plus taxes, whichever is lower. It's usually far cheaper than a transfer.

## The short version

Compare the spread, not just the headline rate. Ask your own bank first. If you do switch, keep the same tenure or EMI, add up every fee, and make sure the savings recover them within a year or two. Late in a loan, or for a small rate gap, staying put is often the better deal.
