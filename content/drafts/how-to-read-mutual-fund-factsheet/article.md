# How to read a mutual fund factsheet: a 10-minute walkthrough

A mutual fund factsheet is the fund house's monthly summary of a scheme: what it's meant to do, what it costs, how it has done against its benchmark, what it owns, and how risky it has been. Most guides explain all fifty terms on it one by one. You don't need all fifty. You need to read about ten of them in the right order, and avoid three or four traps that catch most first-time readers.

This guide works through a real one, the August 2026 factsheet of Parag Parikh Flexi Cap Fund, which managed about ₹1.47 lakh crore at the time. The fund was chosen because its factsheet is detailed and public, not as a recommendation.

## Where to find it, and which month you're reading

Every fund house (AMC) publishes factsheets on its own website, usually under "Downloads", "Factsheets" or "Scheme documents". Most AMCs put all their schemes in one PDF each month. Fund platforms also link to them.

Check the date first. A factsheet shows the position on the last day of a month, and it's published a week or two later. So in early October you'll usually find August's or September's. Everything below comes from the [PPFAS factsheet for August 2026](https://amc.ppfas.com/downloads/factsheet/2026/ppfas-mf-factsheet-for-August-2026.pdf), with figures as on 31 August 2026.

## Step 1: what the fund is allowed to do

Before any numbers, read the four lines that set the rules.

| Field | Parag Parikh Flexi Cap (Aug 2026) | What to take from it |
|---|---|---|
| Type of scheme | Open-ended dynamic equity scheme across large, mid and small caps | Flexi cap: the manager can move between company sizes |
| Investment objective | Long-term capital growth from equity, including foreign equities and debt | This one can hold overseas shares, which many flexi-cap funds can't |
| Benchmark | Nifty 500 TRI | The yardstick for every performance number |
| Riskometer | Very high risk | Same level as its benchmark |

The **objective** is a statement of intent, not a promise; PPFAS's own video makes this point. Its real use is to tell you what the manager may do. Here, the allowance for foreign equities explains why about 11% of the fund sits in shares like Alphabet and Microsoft, which you'd otherwise find surprising.

The **benchmark** ends in "TRI", total return index, meaning it includes dividends. That's the fair comparison; a benchmark without dividends would make any fund look better.

The **riskometer** is SEBI's six-level scale, from "Low" to "Very High". [SEBI's investor website](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/riskometer.html) lists the levels. Under SEBI's [October 2020 circular](https://taxguru.in/sebi/product-labeling-mutual-fund-schemes-risk-o-meter.html), fund houses re-evaluate it every month, publish it with the portfolio within 10 days of the month's end, and must tell investors by email or SMS when a scheme's level changes. Almost every equity fund is "Very High", so for equity funds it mostly tells you that you're in equity.

**Also check:** the fund manager's name and "managing since" date. If the manager changed recently, the long-term track record belongs partly to someone else. This factsheet lists several managers, with the chief investment officer in charge since the fund began in 2013 and three managers (one equity, two debt) added in September 2025.

## Step 2: what it costs

This is where the first trap is.

The factsheet shows a **base expense ratio** of 1.05% for the regular plan and 0.53% for the direct plan, and adds a note that the total expense ratio is on the AMC's website. Those two numbers aren't the same thing. The base figure leaves out items that are charged on top, such as GST on the management fee. The total TER is what actually comes out of your returns.

| | Base expense ratio (factsheet) | Total TER (AMFI, Sep 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Regular plan | 1.05% | 1.30% |
| Direct plan | 0.53% | 0.69% |

*Factsheet as on 31 Aug 2026; total TER from [AMFI's TER disclosures](https://www.amfiindia.com/ter-of-mf-schemes) for 30 Sep 2026.*

So when comparing funds, use the total TER from the AMC's "statutory disclosures" page or AMFI, not the factsheet's base figure. The difference between this fund's regular and direct plans, 0.61 points, works out to about ₹6,100 a year on a ₹10 lakh holding. Small percentages compound into large sums over decades, the same way a slightly higher loan rate does, as we showed in [reading a loan's APR instead of the EMI](https://techpulzo.in/how-to-read-a-loan-apr-instead-of-just-the-emi).

**Exit load** is the other cost. This fund lets you redeem 10% of your units in each purchase free at any time; the rest pays 2% if redeemed within a year and 1% in the second year, then nothing. For example, if you invested ₹1 lakh, it grew to ₹1.1 lakh, and you redeemed everything after ten months, the load would be 2% on ₹99,000 (the 90% that isn't free), or ₹1,980. As PPFAS explains in its video, exit loads go back into the scheme for the remaining investors, not to the fund house.

## Step 3: performance, read correctly

The performance table is the section people read first, and it's where the second trap is: there are separate rows for the regular plan and the direct plan, and they differ.

| Annualised return to 31 Aug 2026 | Regular plan | Direct plan | Nifty 500 TRI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 year | -0.94% | -0.32% | 5.31% |
| 3 years | 13.19% | 13.96% | 12.54% |
| 5 years | 11.59% | 12.46% | 11.12% |
| 10 years | 16.13% | 17.02% | 13.31% |

*Source: PPFAS factsheet, August 2026. Returns over one year are CAGR.*

Three ways to read it:

- **Compare the row for the plan you hold.** The gap between regular and direct here is roughly 0.6–0.9 points a year, which is the cost difference from Step 2 showing up in returns.
- **Compare with the benchmark, not with zero.** Over the last year this fund trailed its benchmark by about 5.6 points (direct plan). Over three, five and ten years it was ahead. Both facts matter; one bad year doesn't define a fund, and neither does one good one.
- **Look at more than one period.** A single 1-year figure depends heavily on the start and end dates.

### Rolling returns: the better test

Some factsheets, including this one, also show **rolling returns**: the return over every possible three-year window since launch, not just the latest one. For this fund, the three-year rolling figures were:

| 3-year rolling return (since launch) | Direct plan | Nifty 500 TRI |
|---|---|---|
| Average | 19.12% | 14.89% |
| Worst three-year window | 0.74% | -6.31% |

*Source: PPFAS factsheet, August 2026, daily rolling.*

This tells you something a point-to-point return can't: how bad the worst stretch was. If your fund's factsheet doesn't show rolling returns, fund research sites usually calculate them.

### SIP returns

The factsheet also shows what a monthly SIP would have returned over the same periods. SIP returns differ from lump-sum returns because each instalment buys at a different price. If you invest monthly, these are the figures closest to your own experience. Our guide to [SIP vs lump-sum investing](https://techpulzo.in/sip-vs-lump-sum-how-to-actually-decide-where-to-put-your-money) explains why they diverge.

## Step 4: what it owns

**Asset allocation** tells you how much is in shares. For this fund in August 2026: about 71% in Indian equity, 11% in overseas shares and around 2% in hedged arbitrage positions, with the rest in REITs and InvITs, short-term debt and cash. The fund's own outlook note says roughly 11.6% sat in cash, debt and arbitrage, ready to invest.

That matters when you compare it with another flexi-cap fund. A fund holding 11% in cash will usually lag in a rising market and fall less in a falling one, so part of any return difference comes from that choice, not from stock-picking.

**Holdings and sectors** show where the money is concentrated. Check what share the top ten holdings make up, and whether one sector, often banks, dominates. If you hold several funds, look for overlap: three funds with the same top five stocks give you less diversification than it seems.

**Market-cap split**, where shown, tells you how much is in large, mid and small companies. As the Groww video's example of a large-cap fund shows, a "large cap" fund can still hold a sixth of its money in mid caps, within the rules.

## Step 5: the risk ratios

This box is the most confusing, and the third trap is reading these numbers on their own. Each one only means something next to the same figure for the benchmark or for similar funds.

| Measure | Parag Parikh Flexi Cap | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Standard deviation | 9.93% | How much returns swing around their average; higher means bumpier |
| Beta | 0.60 | How much the fund moves when its benchmark moves; below 1 means less |
| Sharpe ratio | 0.80 | Return earned above a risk-free rate for each unit of swing; higher is better |
| Information ratio | -0.01 | How consistently it beat its benchmark, after adjusting for how far it strays from it |
| Portfolio turnover | 16.84% (43.00% including arbitrage) | How much of the portfolio was traded in a year |

*Source: PPFAS factsheet, August 2026. Risk-free rate used: 4.97%.*

What to look for:

- **Beta and standard deviation** together tell you how bumpy the ride has been. A beta of 0.60 says this fund has moved much less than the Nifty 500, which fits its cash, overseas and arbitrage holdings.
- **Sharpe ratio** depends on the risk-free rate the AMC assumed (here, the TREPS overnight rate of 4.97%), so compare it only with funds whose factsheets use a similar rate and period.
- **Information ratio** became mandatory under a [SEBI circular of 17 January 2025](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/circulars/jan-2025/disclosure-of-risk-adjusted-return-information-ratio-ir-for-mutual-fund-schemes-_90928.html): every equity-oriented scheme must publish it daily, calculated the same way against its Tier 1 benchmark, and AMFI must make the figures downloadable so you can compare schemes. A positive figure means the fund beat its benchmark relative to how far it strayed from it. The factsheet doesn't always state the period behind the figure; a near-zero reading here sits alongside a fund that lagged over the past year but led over longer periods, so check which window it covers.
- **Portfolio turnover** is shown twice on this factsheet, which PPFAS's video explains: arbitrage trades are bought and sold constantly by design, so the higher figure overstates how often the manager changes core holdings. Lower turnover usually means lower hidden trading costs.

## Debt funds: four different numbers

For debt and liquid funds, the ratios above matter less. Look instead at:

| Measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Yield to maturity (YTM) | The portfolio's current yield if every bond were held to maturity. A guide, not a promised return |
| Modified duration | How much the fund's value may move if interest rates change by 1 point. Higher means more rate risk |
| Average maturity | How long, on average, until the bonds repay |
| Credit rating mix | Share in sovereign (government), AAA and lower-rated paper |

Debt funds also carry a **potential risk class** grid showing credit risk and interest-rate risk separately. A higher YTM usually comes from lower-rated bonds or longer maturities, so read YTM together with the rating mix and duration.

## A 10-minute checklist

1. Check the month the factsheet is for.
2. Read the type, objective and benchmark: is this what you thought you were buying?
3. Note the manager and how long they've run the fund.
4. Get the total TER (not the base figure) for the plan you hold.
5. Read the performance row for your plan, against the benchmark, over 3, 5 and 10 years.
6. Look at rolling returns or the worst period, if shown.
7. Check asset allocation: how much is in equity, and how much in cash.
8. Scan the top holdings and sectors for concentration and overlap with your other funds.
9. Compare beta, standard deviation, Sharpe and information ratio with two or three similar funds.
10. Note the exit load before you redeem or switch.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can I download a mutual fund factsheet?

From the fund house's website, usually under a "Downloads", "Factsheets" or "Scheme documents" section. Most AMCs publish one PDF each month covering all their schemes. Fund platforms and apps often link to the latest one from each scheme's page.

### Is the expense ratio on the factsheet the one I pay?

Not always. Many factsheets show the base expense ratio, which leaves out charges such as GST on the management fee. For Parag Parikh Flexi Cap, the factsheet's 0.53% base figure for the direct plan compares with a total TER of 0.69% in AMFI's September 2026 data. Use the total TER from the AMC's statutory disclosures or AMFI.

### What is a good Sharpe ratio for a mutual fund?

There's no fixed good number, because it depends on the period and the risk-free rate used. Compare a fund's Sharpe ratio with funds in the same category, calculated over the same period. Higher means more return for each unit of volatility.

### What does the information ratio show?

It measures how much a fund beat its benchmark, divided by how much its returns strayed from the benchmark. SEBI has required equity-oriented schemes to disclose it daily since its January 2025 circular, using a standard formula, so you can compare funds on AMFI's website.

### Why do direct and regular plans show different returns?

They hold the same portfolio but charge different expenses. The regular plan includes distributor commission, so its expense ratio is higher and its returns are lower by roughly that difference each year.

## Make it a habit

You don't need to read every factsheet every month. Read the full factsheet once before you invest, then glance at a fund's factsheet once or twice a year: the cost, the performance rows against the benchmark, the manager's name and the cash level. Most changes worth acting on show up in one of those.
