# Return of premium term insurance: what the "refund" really costs you

A return of premium term insurance plan (often sold as TROP) promises the one thing a plain term plan can't: if you're alive at the end, you get your premiums back. It sounds like free insurance. It isn't. You pay a much higher premium every year for that refund, and what you get back decades later is worth far less than it looks.

This guide works through the numbers from real 2026 quotes, shows what the extra premium actually earns, explains the zero-cost term plan alternative, and covers when a refund plan might still suit you.

## How much more it costs

Ditto Insurance's [2026 comparison](https://joinditto.in/term-insurance/term-insurance-with-return-of-premium/) quotes a ₹2 crore cover until age 65 for a male non-smoker:

| Age | Pure term premium | Return of premium premium | Extra each year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | ₹19,719 | ₹47,075 | ₹27,356 (+139%) |
| 30 | ₹25,153 | ₹66,841 | ₹41,688 (+166%) |

The refund at the end is the total of the **base premiums** you paid. It excludes rider premiums, and any extra premium the insurer loaded for your health, as [Bajaj Life's description](https://www.bajajlifeinsurance.com/term-insurance-plans/term-plan-with-return-of-premium.html) of the refund puts it. Life cover is the same in both plans.

**GST is no longer a factor.** Individual life insurance premiums have been exempt from GST since 22 September 2025, under the [GST Council's 56th meeting decisions](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2163555). Older explainers, including Ditto's own [zero-cost term video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0kSKsG1Zb8A), mention losing the 18% GST on refunds; that no longer applies to new premiums.

## What the extra premium earns

Treat the refund plan as two things: a pure term plan, plus a savings scheme where you pay the extra premium each year and get the refund at the end. Then ask what return that savings scheme pays.

For the 30-year-old above, paying ₹41,688 extra every year for 35 years and receiving ₹23.4 lakh (35 × ₹66,841) at 65 works out to about **2.5% a year**. The 25-year-old's figures give the same 2.5%.

| The same extra premium, invested at | Value at 65 (age 30 buyer) |
|---|---|
| 2.5% (what the refund plan pays) | ₹23.4 lakh |
| 6% | ₹49.2 lakh |
| 10% | ₹1.24 crore |

*Our calculation, assuming each year's extra premium is invested at the start of the year.*

Inflation makes the refund smaller still. ₹23.4 lakh received 35 years from now is worth about ₹4.2 lakh in today's money at 5% inflation. Ditto's article runs the same comparison at 6% and reaches a similar conclusion.

Gurleen Kaur Tikku's [spreadsheet comparison](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1-x5wIc-D5o) (in Hindi) makes the same case: buy the pure term plan and invest the difference.

## The catch if you stop paying

A pure term plan has no exit cost: if you stop paying, the cover simply ends. A refund plan does. If you stop early, you don't get your premiums back; you get a **surrender value**, which Ditto notes is generally lower than the full refund, especially in the early years. Because you're paying two or three times the premium, a job loss or a squeezed budget is also more likely to make you lapse, and the lapse costs more.

## The cheaper alternative: zero-cost term plans

Several insurers now offer term plans with a built-in **exit option**: at a set point (for example after 25 years or at age 60), you can end the policy and get your base premiums back. Ditto's [explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0kSKsG1Zb8A) points out that these are usually priced close to ordinary term plans, unlike return of premium plans, which cost much more.

The same video flags the catch: to qualify for the exit refund, some insurers require a long policy term, to age 75 or 80. If you only need cover until 65, stretching the term just to get the refund means paying for cover you don't need. Check the conditions before choosing one for the refund.

## When a refund plan might still make sense

- **You won't invest the difference.** If the alternative is spending it, a forced 2.5% is better than nothing, but a PPF account or recurring deposit is usually a better forced saving.
- **Peace of mind matters more to you than return**, and the extra premium fits comfortably in your budget for the whole term.
- **Tax.** Premiums can qualify for the old regime's 80C deduction, and the maturity refund is generally tax-free subject to conditions, as Bajaj Life notes. That helps, but it doesn't change a 2.5% return into a good one.

For most buyers, the better plan is a pure term policy for the cover you need, worked out properly (see our guide to [budgeting](https://techpulzo.in/zero-based-budgeting-how-to-build-a-budget-that-actually-works) to find room for it), with the difference invested separately.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is return of premium term insurance worth it?

Usually not. In 2026 quotes for a ₹2 crore cover, the return of premium version costs 139–166% more than pure term, and the extra premium earns only about 2.5% a year through the refund. Investing the difference separately, even at a modest 6%, leaves you with roughly twice as much.

### What do you get back in a return of premium term plan?

The total base premiums you paid, if you survive the policy term. Rider premiums and any extra premium charged for your health aren't refunded, and there's no interest or bonus.

### Is GST refunded in a TROP plan?

GST no longer applies. Individual life insurance premiums have been exempt from GST since 22 September 2025, so premiums paid since then don't include it.

### What is a zero-cost term plan?

A term plan with an exit option: at a set age or policy year you can end the cover and get your base premiums back. It's usually priced close to a normal term plan, but some require a longer policy term to qualify for the refund.

### What happens if I stop paying a return of premium plan?

You get a surrender value or a reduced paid-up policy, depending on the plan, which is generally less than the premiums you paid, especially in the early years. A pure term plan simply ends.

## The short version

A return of premium plan charges you two to three times the premium to give back, decades later, money that has earned about 2.5% a year. Buy pure term cover for what you need and invest the difference; if you want a refund option, compare zero-cost exit plans, but don't stretch the term to qualify.
