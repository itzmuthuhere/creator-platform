# Credit utilisation ratio: how much of your card limit to use, and when it's measured

Your credit utilisation ratio (often spelled credit utilization ratio) is the share of your credit card limit you're using: ₹30,000 owed on a ₹1 lakh limit is 30%. It's one of the main inputs to your CIBIL score, and unlike payment history, which takes years to repair, it can change within weeks. Most advice stops at "keep it under 30%". What helps more is knowing which balance gets reported, when, and the few moves that lower the number without spending less.

This guide covers how the ratio is worked out, why paying in full doesn't always mean a low ratio, how India's fortnightly reporting changes the timing, and what to do about it. For the wider picture of how scores work, see our guide to [credit scores in India](https://techpulzo.in/credit-score-in-india-how-it-works-and-how-to-fix-it).

## How the ratio is worked out

**Utilisation = card balances ÷ card limits.** TransUnion CIBIL lists credit utilisation among the main factors in its score, next to payment history, and in [its own guidance](https://www.cibil.com/blog/six-ways-you-can-use-credit-card-for-optimum-credit-score) calls keeping your **overall** card spend to 30% of your limit "a healthy ratio".

"Overall" matters. With three cards of ₹1 lakh each, a total limit of ₹3 lakh, 30% is ₹90,000 across all of them, as IT Masti's [explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sJfwGvrOxS8) works out. Utkarsh Ojha's [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jXdKQY-sc_M) shows this in a real credit report: one of his cards was above 50% used, but the report showed his overall utilisation at about 12%, because his other cards were barely used.

That said, your report lists every card with its own balance and limit. A lender reading it can see one card near its limit, even when your overall figure looks fine.

Utilisation is about **revolving credit**, meaning credit cards and credit lines. EMIs on a home or car loan are judged differently, by how reliably you pay them.

## Why "I pay in full" can still show high utilisation

The bureau doesn't see your spending; it sees the **balance your card issuer reports** on its reporting dates. Since January 2025, the RBI has required banks and other lenders to report credit information to bureaus **every fortnight, as on the 15th and the last day of each month**, within seven days, and bureaus must load it within five days, as [TaxGuru's summary of the RBI circular](https://taxguru.in/rbi/rbi-mandates-fortnightly-credit-info-reporting-cics-cis.html) sets out.

So if you put ₹70,000 on a ₹1 lakh card and pay the whole bill on its due date, the balance on a reporting date in between may well show ₹70,000, which is 70%, even though you never paid a rupee of interest. Exactly which balance each issuer reports (the outstanding on that date or the last statement balance) isn't published, but either way a large balance outstanding around those dates shows up.

## Example: same spending, different ratio

Meena has two cards: Card A with a ₹1 lakh limit and Card B with ₹50,000. She spends ₹45,000 a month, all on Card A, and pays in full every month.

| What she does | Card A | Card B | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything on Card A, paid on the due date | 45% | 0% | **30%** |
| Splits spending ₹30,000 / ₹15,000 | 30% | 30% | 30% |
| Pays ₹30,000 early, before the 15th | 15% | 0% | **10%** |
| Card A limit raised to ₹2 lakh | 22.5% | 0% | **18%** |

The spending is identical in every row. Paying part of the balance before a reporting date makes the biggest difference, and costs nothing.

## Ways to lower your ratio without spending less

1. **Pay before the reporting date, not just by the due date.** A mid-cycle payment before the 15th or the month-end cuts the balance the bureau sees.
2. **Move your billing cycle.** Under the [RBI's 2022 credit card rules](https://www.mondaq.com/india/financial-services/1214312/master-directions-on-credit-card-and-debit-card-issuance-and-conduct-directions-2022), you get a one-time option to change your card's billing cycle. A statement that closes and is paid well before a reporting date helps.
3. **Spread large spends** across cards, or time a big purchase so it's paid off before the next reporting date.
4. **Ask for a higher limit**, but only if you won't spend into it. The same RBI rules say an issuer can't exceed your sanctioned limit without your **explicit consent**, so limit increases happen only when you agree to them.
5. **Keep old, unused cards open** if they have no annual fee. Closing one removes its limit from your total and raises your ratio overnight. If a card has a fee you don't want to pay, closing it is still fine; the RBI rules require issuers to close a card within seven working days once dues are paid.

What not to do: open new cards just to raise your total limit. Each application adds an enquiry to your report, and CIBIL's guidance warns that too many cards add to your credit exposure.

## How low is low enough?

CIBIL's guidance points to 30% overall. Lower is generally better. But the ratio is only one factor: **paying every bill on time matters more**, and a score doesn't improve because you use 0%. Some use, paid in full, shows you can handle credit.

Because bureaus now get data every two weeks, a high month isn't permanent. Pay the balance down and the next reports reflect it, often within a month.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good credit utilisation ratio in India?

TransUnion CIBIL describes keeping your overall credit card spend within 30% of your limit as healthy. Lower is better, as long as you pay every bill on time, which matters more than the ratio itself.

### Is credit utilisation calculated per card or across all cards?

The headline ratio is across all your cards: total balances divided by total limits. Your credit report still shows each card's balance and limit separately, so a single card near its limit is visible to lenders.

### Does paying my credit card bill in full keep utilisation low?

Not necessarily. Lenders report your balance to the bureaus as on the 15th and the last day of each month. If a large balance is outstanding on those dates, it shows as high utilisation even if you pay in full by the due date. Paying part of it earlier lowers what's reported.

### Will increasing my credit limit improve my CIBIL score?

It can lower your utilisation ratio if your spending stays the same. Card issuers need your explicit consent to raise your limit. Don't apply for new cards just to add limit; each application adds an enquiry.

### Does closing a credit card hurt my credit score?

It can raise your utilisation, because that card's limit no longer counts towards your total. If the card has no fee, keeping it open and occasionally using it is usually better for your ratio.

## The short version

Keep total card balances under about 30% of total limits, and remember the bureau sees your balance on the 15th and at month-end, not your spending. Paying part of a big bill early, spreading spends, and keeping fee-free old cards open all lower the ratio without changing what you buy.
