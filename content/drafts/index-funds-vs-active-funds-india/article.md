# Index funds vs active funds in India: what the 2026 data says

Two numbers settle most of this debate. The first is the odds: over the ten years to June 2026, 74% of Indian large-cap funds did worse than their benchmark, according to S&P Dow Jones Indices. The second is the cost: the typical active large-cap fund charges about 0.8 percentage points a year more than a Nifty 50 index fund, even in direct plans. An active fund has to beat the market by that much every year just to break even with the cheap option.

That doesn't mean every active fund is a bad idea. It means the default should be an index fund, and an active fund needs a reason. This guide sets out the evidence, works out what the cost gap is worth in rupees, and covers where active funds still have a case.

## What the two choices are

An **index fund** buys every stock in an index, such as the Nifty 50, in the same proportions, and changes its holdings only when the index changes. Nobody is picking stocks, so the fund costs little to run and its returns track the index, minus a small fee.

An **active fund** employs a fund manager and research team to choose stocks they expect to do better than the index. You pay more for that, through the expense ratio (TER), and you get either more or less than the index depending on how well they choose.

So the question is simple: after costs, do active managers in India beat their index often enough, and predictably enough, to be worth paying for?

## The odds: SPIVA India, mid-year 2026

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes a scorecard twice a year, called SPIVA, comparing Indian mutual funds with their benchmarks. Its [mid-year 2026 report](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-india/), published on 21 September 2026, found:

| Fund category | Benchmark | Underperformed over 3 years | 5 years | 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large-cap equity | S&P India LargeMidCap | 80% | 85% | 74% |
| ELSS (tax-saving) | S&P India BMI | 64% | 71% | 80% |
| Mid/small-cap equity | S&P India SmallCap | 53% | 55% | 82% |

*Share of active funds that returned less than their benchmark, to 30 June 2026. Source: SPIVA India Mid-Year 2026.*

Over the first half of 2026 alone, 85% of large-cap funds lagged their benchmark, as did 55% of ELSS funds and 67% of mid/small-cap funds.

Three things stand out:

- **Large-cap is the hardest place to win.** The 50 or 100 biggest Indian companies are followed by every analyst in the country, so there's little an active manager knows that the market doesn't. Four out of five large-cap funds lost over three and five years.
- **Mid and small caps look better over short periods, worse over long ones.** Around half of mid/small-cap funds beat their benchmark over three and five years, but 82% lagged over ten. Some short-term winners don't last.
- **Tax-saving ELSS funds are mostly active, and most of them lag.** Eight in ten fell behind over ten years.

### What other studies found

The YouTube channel moniwise compared 23 active mid-cap funds with a Nifty Midcap 150 index fund over six years to September 2025. In [their analysis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwmoMrRAHvc), 13 of the 23 beat the index over one year, but only 8 did over three, five and six years, and only about four did so consistently across every period they checked. That's a closer contest than large caps, which matches the SPIVA picture for shorter horizons.

M Pattabiraman of freefincal argues in [his video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gHnWZn5wY1o) that roughly half of active funds beat a reasonable benchmark, but that the winners change from one period to the next. That second point is the real problem: even where some funds do beat the index, you have to pick them in advance, not afterwards. The SPIVA ten-year figures suggest his "half" is generous for India over long periods.

## The cost hurdle: what 0.8 percentage points is worth

We pulled every scheme's expense ratio from [AMFI's TER disclosures](https://www.amfiindia.com/ter-of-mf-schemes) for September 2026 and compared direct plans (the cheaper version with no distributor commission):

| Fund type (direct plan) | Median TER |
|---|---|
| Plain Nifty 50 index funds (32 funds) | 0.25% (cheapest: 0.10%) |
| Active large-cap funds | 1.06% |
| Active flexi-cap funds | 0.92% |
| Active mid-cap funds | 0.96% |
| Active small-cap funds | 0.91% |

*Median annual expense ratio, AMFI data for September 2026. Our analysis.*

The gap between a median Nifty 50 index fund and a median active large-cap fund is about 0.81 percentage points a year. That sounds small. Over a long SIP, it isn't:

**Example.** You invest ₹10,000 a month for 20 years, and the market returns 12% a year before fees.

| | Index fund (0.25% TER) | Active fund (1.06% TER), matching the market before fees |
|---|---|---|
| Total invested | ₹24 lakh | ₹24 lakh |
| Value after 20 years | about ₹89.3 lakh | about ₹81.0 lakh |
| Difference | | **about ₹8.3 lakh less** |

*Monthly compounding. Our calculation.*

The active fund has to beat the market by about 0.8 points every year, for 20 years, just to end level with the index fund. It's the same compounding effect that makes a small difference in a loan's interest rate matter more than it looks, which we covered in [reading a loan's APR instead of the EMI](https://techpulzo.in/how-to-read-a-loan-apr-instead-of-just-the-emi).

If you hold the regular plan of an active fund, through a bank or distributor, the hurdle is higher still, because regular plans add commission on top.

## Where active funds still have a case

The data argues for index funds as the default, not as the only answer. Active funds are easier to justify where:

- **The index is narrow or hard to replicate.** Small-cap indices hold many illiquid stocks, and small-cap index funds can track their index less closely. Over three and five years, about half of active mid/small-cap funds beat the benchmark in the SPIVA data, so this is the most contested area.
- **You want a style the plain indices don't give you.** Value, contra or focused funds are active by design. If that's the exposure you want, an index fund isn't a substitute, though you should judge the fund against a fair benchmark rather than its own marketing.
- **You're already happy with a fund you've held for years.** Switching out of an equity fund can mean capital gains tax and an exit load. Moving new SIPs to an index fund and leaving the old units alone is often the cheaper way to change course.

What doesn't count as a reason: a fund's last one-year return, its star rating, or a recommendation from someone paid commission to sell it.

## Choosing an index fund

Index funds that track the same index are close substitutes, so the choice comes down to a few checks:

1. **Pick a broad, plain index.** Nifty 50 or Sensex for large caps; Nifty 100, Nifty 500 or a Total Market index for wider coverage. Freefincal's video points out that the Nifty 500 is still roughly two-thirds large cap by weight, so it is less of a mid/small-cap bet than its name suggests. It also advises against "smart beta" indices (momentum, low volatility, quality and the like), which are rules-based strategies that can trail the plain index for long stretches.
2. **Look at the expense ratio of the direct plan.** For Nifty 50 funds, anything near 0.10–0.25% is normal. There's no reason to pay more for the same index.
3. **Check the tracking error and tracking difference.** Tracking error shows how closely the fund's daily returns follow its index; tracking difference shows how far its returns fall short of the index over a period. Lower is better for both. Under SEBI's 2022 rules for passive funds, [as Moneylife reported](https://www.moneylife.in/article/sebi-tracking-error-of-equity-etfs-and-index-funds-cannot-exceed-2-percentage/67279.html), equity index funds and ETFs must keep tracking error under 2%, publish it daily on the AMC and AMFI websites, and publish tracking difference monthly.
4. **Prefer a fund with reasonable size.** Larger funds generally track more closely and are less likely to be merged away.
5. **Index fund or ETF?** An ETF holds the same index but trades on the stock exchange, so you need a demat account and you buy at market prices, sometimes away from the true value. For a monthly SIP, an index fund is simpler, which is also freefincal's preference.

## A sensible split

For most people building wealth through SIPs:

- **Core (most of your equity):** one or two broad index funds, such as a Nifty 50 fund plus a Nifty Next 50 or Midcap 150 index fund, or a single Nifty 500 / Total Market fund.
- **Optional satellite:** if you want an active fund, limit it to a small share and to a category where the evidence is mixed (mid or small caps), and judge it against its index over five years, not one.

How you invest matters more than which kind of fund. Freefincal's broader point is worth repeating: your goals, how much equity you hold and whether you keep investing through downturns will matter far more than the active-versus-passive choice. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do active mutual funds beat index funds in India?

Most don't over long periods. The SPIVA India mid-year 2026 report found that 74% of large-cap funds, 80% of ELSS funds and 82% of mid/small-cap funds trailed their benchmarks over ten years to June 2026. Over three to five years, mid and small caps were closer to an even contest.

### Why are index funds cheaper?

There's no stock-picking team to pay and little trading, because the fund simply holds the index. In September 2026, the median direct-plan Nifty 50 index fund charged 0.25% a year, against 1.06% for the median active large-cap fund, according to AMFI data.

### Which index fund is best for a beginner?

A low-cost Nifty 50 or Sensex index fund in the direct plan is the simplest start. Compare expense ratios and tracking error among funds on the same index, and choose a reasonably large fund. If you want wider coverage, a Nifty 500 or Total Market index fund adds mid and small caps in one holding.

### Are index funds safer than active funds?

No. An index fund falls as much as the market does, and there's no manager trying to cushion it. It removes the risk of picking the wrong fund manager, not the risk of the market itself.

### Should I switch my existing active funds to index funds?

Not necessarily all at once. Selling equity units can trigger capital gains tax and, for recent purchases, an exit load. A common approach is to direct new SIPs into index funds and review the old holdings against their benchmark over five years.

## The short answer

For most Indian investors, a low-cost index fund should be the default: the odds favour it over ten years in every category SPIVA measures, and the cost gap alone is worth lakhs over a long SIP. Use active funds deliberately, in small amounts and in the corners of the market where the evidence is mixed, and check them against their index rather than against last year's return.
