# NPS explained: tiers, tax benefits and what you get at 60

The National Pension System is two things at once: a low-cost retirement account invested in equity and bonds, and a tax tool. Which NPS tax benefit you can use depends on your tax regime. The well-known extra ₹50,000 deduction works only in the old regime; in the new regime, the benefit that matters is your employer's contribution. And the rules for taking money out changed sharply in December 2025, mostly in subscribers' favour, with one tax catch most explainers miss.

## What NPS is

NPS is a defined-contribution pension account regulated by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA). You (and often your employer) contribute; the money is invested by a pension fund manager you choose, across equity, corporate bonds and government bonds; and at retirement you take part of it as a lump sum and use the rest for a pension.

It has two accounts:

| | Tier I | Tier II |
|---|---|---|
| Required? | Yes; this is the pension account | Optional; needs a Tier I account first |
| Money locked? | Yes, until exit (with limited withdrawals) | No; withdraw any time |
| Tax benefits | The deductions below | Generally none, except a "Tax Saver" version with a 3-year lock-in, per [ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/nps-national-pension-scheme) |

For tax and retirement purposes, everything below is about Tier I.

## The three tax benefits, and which regime they belong to

| Benefit | What it is | Old regime | New regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your own contribution, within the ₹1.5 lakh investment limit | Counts towards the same ₹1.5 lakh as EPF, PPF and ELSS | Yes | No |
| **Extra ₹50,000** for your own contribution | Over and above the ₹1.5 lakh | Yes | No |
| **Employer's contribution** | Deductible up to a share of your salary (basic + DA) | Up to 10% | Up to 14% |

*Limits as summarised by ClearTax for 2026-27.*

So the extra ₹50,000 that NPS is famous for only helps if you file under the old regime. For the large majority now on the new regime, the only NPS deduction left is the one your employer's contribution creates, and it's worth more than the ₹50,000 for most salaried people.

## How much each saves

**Employer contribution, new regime.** If your employer agrees to route 14% of your basic salary into NPS as part of your CTC (assuming basic is half of CTC):

| CTC | Employer NPS (14% of basic) | Tax without | Tax with | Saving each year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ₹12 lakh | ₹84,000 | ₹0 | ₹0 | ₹0 |
| ₹15 lakh | ₹1,05,000 | ₹97,500 | ₹81,120 | ₹16,380 |
| ₹20 lakh | ₹1,40,000 | ₹1,92,400 | ₹1,63,280 | ₹29,120 |
| ₹30 lakh | ₹2,10,000 | ₹4,75,800 | ₹4,10,280 | ₹65,520 |

*Tax year 2026-27, including cess. Our calculation.*

Up to about ₹12.75 lakh the new regime already charges no tax, so the contribution saves nothing there. Above that, it's one of the few levers left. ET Money's explainer on corporate NPS walks through asking your employer to restructure your salary this way.

**The extra ₹50,000, old regime.** It saves ₹2,600 a year at the 5% slab, ₹10,400 at 20% and ₹15,600 at 30%, including cess.

CA Niraj Dugar notes one ceiling for high earners: combined employer contributions to EPF, NPS and superannuation above ₹7.5 lakh a year become taxable. That matters only at quite high salaries.

## What you get at 60 under the new rules

PFRDA rewrote its [exit and withdrawal regulations](https://www.pfrda.org.in/documents/33652/184762/PFRDA+(Exits+and+Withdrawals+under+the+NPS)+Regulations,+2015+%5BLast+amended+on+16+December+2025%5D.pdf) on 16 December 2025. For non-government subscribers at normal exit (age 60, or after 15 years in the scheme):

| Corpus at exit | What you can do |
|---|---|
| Up to ₹8 lakh | Take all of it as a lump sum |
| ₹8–12 lakh | Up to ₹6 lakh as a lump sum; the rest as regular payouts over at least six years, or an annuity |
| Above ₹12 lakh | Up to **80%** as a lump sum; at least **20%** must buy an annuity (a lifelong pension), as [Protean](https://www.proteantech.in/articles/nps-withdrawal-rules-2025-changes-december/) summarises |

Before December 2025 the split was 60% lump sum and 40% annuity. Some recent explainers, including one published only a month ago, still describe that older split. You can also put off both the lump sum and the annuity until age 85, and the 15-year exit option applies to accounts you open yourself, not to employer-linked ones.

Government employees remain on the 60/40 split.

## The tax catch on the 80%

The new rules let you *withdraw* 80%, but the tax law still exempts only **60%** of the corpus, under section 10(12A). As [1 Finance explained in May 2026](https://1finance.co.in/blog/new-nps-withdrawal-rules-taxation/), after this year's budget, the extra 20% you take out is taxed at your slab rate in the year you withdraw it, and the annuity's monthly pension is taxed as you receive it. CA Niraj Dugar's video describes the full 80% as tax-free; that isn't the case unless the law changes.

An example with a ₹1 crore corpus at 60:

| Part | Amount | Tax |
|---|---|---|
| Tax-free lump sum (60%) | ₹60 lakh | None |
| Extra lump sum (20%) | ₹20 lakh | Taxed at your slab that year |
| Annuity purchase (20%) | ₹20 lakh | None at purchase; pension taxed as received |

*Our calculation.*

One way to soften the tax on the extra 20% is to take it over more than one year, using the deferment option. Speak to a tax adviser before you exit, because the right split depends on your other income in those years.

## Getting money out before 60

NPS is meant to stay put, but there are three routes:

- **Partial withdrawals:** up to 25% of your own contributions, for purposes such as children's education or marriage, buying a house, or treatment of illness, at most four times before 60 with at least four years between them, under PFRDA's rules.
- **A loan against NPS:** Protean reports that you can now pledge NPS for a loan of up to 25% of your own contributions.
- **Leaving early:** possible, but a much larger share of the corpus must then buy an annuity; this is the least attractive route.

If you need money you can reach easily, keep it outside Tier I. That's what an emergency fund or ordinary investments are for.

## Is NPS right for you?

- **New regime, salary above about ₹13 lakh, employer offers corporate NPS:** usually yes, at least for the employer route. The tax saving is immediate and the money builds your retirement.
- **Old regime, already using the ₹1.5 lakh limit:** the extra ₹50,000 is a straightforward saving, worth ₹10,000–15,600 a year in the higher slabs.
- **You want flexibility:** NPS is a poor place for anything you might need before 60. Mutual funds through a regular SIP give you full access; our comparison of [SIP and lump-sum investing](https://techpulzo.in/sip-vs-lump-sum-how-to-actually-decide-where-to-put-your-money) covers that side.
- **You'd rather not buy an annuity:** at least 20% of a corpus above ₹12 lakh will still go into one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I claim the extra ₹50,000 NPS deduction in the new tax regime?

No. The extra ₹50,000 for your own NPS contribution is available only under the old regime. In the new regime, the only NPS deduction is for your employer's contribution, up to 14% of your basic salary plus DA. If you've moved to the new regime, ask whether your employer offers corporate NPS.

### Is the NPS lump sum at 60 tax-free?

Only 60% of the corpus is tax-free. The December 2025 rules let non-government subscribers withdraw up to 80% as a lump sum, but the tax exemption wasn't extended; the extra 20% is taxed at your slab rate, and the annuity's pension is taxed as income when you receive it.

### How much of my NPS must go into an annuity now?

For non-government subscribers with more than ₹12 lakh at exit, at least 20%. With ₹8–12 lakh, you can take up to ₹6 lakh as a lump sum and spread the rest over at least six years or buy an annuity. With ₹8 lakh or less, you can withdraw everything. Government employees still annuitise at least 40%.

### What's the difference between NPS Tier I and Tier II?

Tier I is the locked pension account that carries the tax benefits. Tier II is an optional, flexible account you can withdraw from any time, but it generally has no tax benefit, except a Tier II "Tax Saver" variant with a three-year lock-in. You need a Tier I account to open Tier II.

### Can I withdraw from NPS before 60?

Partly. You can withdraw up to 25% of your own contributions for specified purposes, up to four times before 60 with four-year gaps, and you can now take a loan against NPS. Accounts opened by individuals can also exit normally after 15 years. Leaving earlier than that means a much larger share goes into an annuity.

## Pick the benefit that matches your regime

If you're on the new regime, the NPS question is really a salary-structure question: ask HR whether 14% of your basic can go into corporate NPS. If you're on the old regime, the extra ₹50,000 is the easy win. Either way, plan for the 60%/20%/20% tax split at retirement, not the headline 80%. It's also worth keeping your monthly budget in order first; a [zero-based budget](https://techpulzo.in/zero-based-budgeting-how-to-build-a-budget-that-actually-works) makes room for a regular contribution.
