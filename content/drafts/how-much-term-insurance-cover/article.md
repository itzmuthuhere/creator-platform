# How much term insurance cover you need, worked out

Is ₹1 crore enough? For a 32-year-old earning ₹18 lakh with a young child and a home loan, the popular rules of thumb give answers anywhere from ₹1.8 crore to ₹4.5 crore. That's not a rounding difference. One of those numbers leaves a family short; another costs years of extra premium for cover nobody needed.

This article works out how much term insurance cover that household needs, line by line, so you can redo it with your own numbers in about fifteen minutes. Then it covers the limits insurers apply and the mistakes that make a claim go wrong.

## Why the rules of thumb disagree

Ask around and you'll hear three different multiples of annual income:

- **10×**, which Manish Alagh describes in a Smart Up interview as the usual thumb rule for someone around 30, falling to about 5× near retirement.
- **10–20×**, the range Axis Max Life's guide on [choosing a sum assured](https://www.axismaxlife.com/blog/term-insurance/how-to-choose-the-right-sum-assured-under-a-term-plan) attributes to financial planners.
- **20–30×**, the rule of thumb in insurance broker Ditto's guide to term insurance.

They disagree because income is only a rough stand-in for what your family would lose. Two people on the same salary can need very different cover. One may rent, have no loans and a working spouse. The other may be the sole earner with a home loan and two children. A multiple can't see any of that. A short calculation can.

## Working out your number

The idea is simple: the payout should let your family keep living roughly as they do now, clear the debts, and still pay for the big goals you had planned, until the point where you would have stopped earning anyway.

Here is the calculation for our example: age 32, income ₹18 lakh a year, a non-working spouse, a two-year-old child.

**1. The family's yearly spending without you.** Household spending is ₹70,000 a month, of which about ₹10,000 is your own (commute, lunches, your phone, your hobbies). The family would need about ₹60,000 a month, or ₹7.2 lakh a year. If you have a list of fixed costs from a [budget](https://techpulzo.in/zero-based-budgeting-how-to-build-a-budget-that-actually-works), start from that.

**2. For how many years.** Until the age you'd have stopped working, here 60: that's 28 years. By then the child is independent, and your retirement savings would have taken over anyway.

**3. What a lump sum must hold to pay that.** The family's spending rises with inflation, while the payout earns interest. Assuming 6% inflation and a conservative 7% return after tax (mostly debt funds and deposits, because the family can't afford big swings), the lump sum that pays ₹7.2 lakh this year, rising 6% a year for 28 years, is about **₹1.78 crore**.

**4. Add the debts.** The home loan has ₹45 lakh outstanding. Paying it off removes the family's largest fixed cost; our [home loan affordability guide](https://techpulzo.in/how-much-home-loan-you-can-actually-afford) explains why that EMI is often the biggest single commitment. If you'd rather the family keep paying EMIs, leave the loan out but keep the EMI inside step 1.

**5. Add the big goals.** The child's higher education and other planned big expenses, say ₹30 lakh in today's money needed in about 16 years, are worth about ₹25.8 lakh today at a 1% real return.

**6. Add a buffer** of ₹5 lakh for final expenses and the months before the claim pays out.

**7. Subtract what the family already has.** EPF, mutual funds and savings the family could use: ₹15 lakh. Don't subtract your employer's group cover (more on that below).

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Family expenses for 28 years | ₹1.78 crore |
| Home loan outstanding | ₹45 lakh |
| Child's goals (present value) | ₹25.8 lakh |
| Buffer | ₹5 lakh |
| Less investments | −₹15 lakh |
| **Cover needed** | **₹2.39 crore** |

*Assumes 6% inflation and a 7% post-tax return. Our calculation.*

That's about 13 times income: well above the 10× rule, and well below 20–30×. In practice you'd round to the nearest size insurers sell, here ₹2.5 crore.

## What moves the number most

**The return you assume.** It's the biggest lever, and the reason to stay conservative:

| Post-tax return on the payout (6% inflation) | Family-expense part of the cover |
|---|---|
| 6% | ₹2.02 crore |
| 7% | ₹1.78 crore |
| 8% | ₹1.58 crore |

A grieving family managing a lump sum alone shouldn't have to chase equity returns to make it last, so assume a modest return.

**How long dependents need you.** A child of 2 needs cover for 20+ years; a child of 14 needs far less. If your parents depend on you, include their costs for the years they're likely to need support.

**Your spouse's income.** If your spouse earns, count only the gap their income wouldn't cover. Two earners often each need much less than a single earner does.

**What you already own.** Every rupee of savings the family could use reduces the cover rupee for rupee, which is why the need falls as you get older.

## Why one level cover bought young still fits later

Most term plans pay a fixed amount for the whole term. That looks like a problem: ₹2.39 crore buys less in 13 years than it does today.

Run the same calculation for the same household at age 45, though. The loan is down to ₹20 lakh, the child's big goal is three years away, investments have grown to ₹60 lakh, and spending has risen with inflation. The need comes to about **₹2.39 crore in that year's rupees**, roughly the same nominal figure, which is about half the cover in today's money. The family needs less real cover each year because there are fewer years to fund and more savings to draw on, and that decline roughly offsets inflation eating into the fixed sum.

That's why a single level cover, sized properly when you're young, usually stays adequate. You need to revisit it when something big changes: a second child, a larger home loan, a parent becoming dependent. Some plans include a "life stage" option to raise cover at such events, as Ditto's guide notes. Otherwise you can buy a second, smaller policy.

## Limits and traps

### Insurers cap cover by age and income

You can't buy unlimited cover. Each insurer sets a maximum as a multiple of income under its own board-approved underwriting policy. Ditto's [reference table](https://joinditto.in/articles/life-insurance/maximum-sum-assured-in-term-insurance/) puts typical limits at 25–35× income up to age 35, 20× in the early forties and 10× after 50, though exact figures vary by insurer. A needs-based number of around 13× fits comfortably for most young earners. The cap mainly matters if you start late or your needs are unusually large.

### Your employer's group cover doesn't count

Many employers give group life cover, often 3–5 times salary according to Ditto's guide. It ends when you leave the job, and you may not be insurable on the same terms later. Treat it as a bonus, not part of your plan.

### Choose the term by your dependents, not by the advertisement

Cover is needed while someone relies on your income. XY Axis Education's video compares a ₹2 crore plan to age 65 with the same plan to age 99, quoting premiums of ₹1,666 and ₹3,978 a month for a 31-year-old. Unless you have a lifelong dependent, paying more than double for cover into your nineties rarely makes sense; the money does more invested. The same applies to "return of premium" plans, which charge far more for the same cover.

### Disclose everything, and know how claims work

The surest way to put a claim at risk is a gap between the proposal form and the truth: smoking, drinking, existing conditions, other policies. As Ditto's guide puts it, the rejection hits your family, not you. IRDAI's [2024 master circular on protecting policyholders](https://www.caalley.com/irdai_mc/MC_Protection_of_Policyholders_interests_2024.pdf) gives families strong protections, but they assume honest disclosure:

- A death claim must be settled **within 15 days** of being reported, or **45 days** if the insurer needs to investigate. Delays earn interest at the bank rate plus 2%.
- A claim can't be rejected because it was reported late or a document was missing.
- After **three years** from the policy start (or revival), the policy can't be contested on any ground.

Some older guides, including Ditto's video, still quote 30 and 120 days; those were the earlier timelines.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is ₹1 crore term insurance enough?

For a single person without dependents, or a high-earning household with large savings, possibly. For most young families with a home loan and children, no. In our example a ₹18 lakh earner needed about ₹2.4 crore. Run the seven steps above with your own spending, loans and goals, and get quotes for the number you arrive at.

### What's the difference between this method and human life value?

Human life value (HLV) estimates your future income's worth to your family: the present value of what you'd earn, minus your own consumption. The method here works from the other side, starting from what your family would spend, owe and need for goals. The expense-based version is easier to check, because you can see every line.

### Should I subtract my employer's group insurance?

No. Group cover usually ends when you change jobs or are laid off, which is exactly when your family is most exposed. Count only policies you own and pay for yourself. If your employer cover is large, treat it as extra protection while it lasts.

### Should I buy one big policy or two smaller ones?

One level policy sized for your needs is simplest and usually adequate, for the reasons above. Some people split it, for example one policy to 60 and a second, shorter one that ends when the home loan or children's dependence ends, to cut premiums. That works if you set it up deliberately; get quotes for both structures and compare the total premium.

### Until what age should my term cover run?

Until the people who depend on you no longer need your income: typically when your children are independent and your retirement savings could support your spouse, often around 60–65. Longer only if you have a lifelong dependent or expect to work much later.

## Fifteen minutes, then revisit at each milestone

The calculation needs only six numbers: what your family spends without you, for how many years, your loans, your big goals, your savings and a sensible return. Do it once now, write it down, and redo it when you marry, have a child, take a bigger loan or someone new starts depending on you. The answer will tell you whether to add cover, and by how much.
