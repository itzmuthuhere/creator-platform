# Repo rate hike or cut: what it does to your home loan EMI

After a repo rate hike, the headlines say your home loan EMI will rise; after a cut, that it will fall. For most floating-rate home loans the change does reach you, but not always as a different EMI, not on the same day, and not by the same amount for everyone. Often the bank quietly changes the **number** of EMIs instead, and that can cost far more than a slightly bigger instalment.

This guide explains how the repo rate flows into your loan rate, what a 0.25% move does in rupees, why your bank usually stretches the tenure, and what you can ask for instead.

## From repo rate to your loan rate

The repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends to banks. Since October 2019, new floating-rate home loans from banks must be linked to an **external benchmark**, such as the repo rate, which is what banks like SBI use, as [SCC Online's summary](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2019/09/07/rbi-makes-external-benchmark-based-interest-rate-mandatory-for-certain-categories-of-loans-from-october-1-2019/) of the RBI circular explains. Your rate is built in layers:

**Your rate = repo rate + bank's markup (the EBLR) + your spread**

SBI is a clear example. Its [published EBLR](https://sbi.bank.in/web/interest-rates/external-benchmark-based-lending-rate) from 15 December 2025 is **7.90% + CRP + BSP**. With the repo at 5.25%, that's a 2.65 percentage-point markup, plus a **credit risk premium** (CRP) set by your credit profile and a **business strategy premium** (BSP) that can be a discount.

When the repo moves by 0.25%, the EBLR moves by the same 0.25%, and so does your rate. Your spread stays where it is.

**When it changes.** Under the external-benchmark rules, the rate on these loans has to be reset **at least once every three months**, [Personal Finance Plan notes](https://personalfinanceplan.in/retail-home-loans-external-benchmark/). So a repo change reaches your loan at the next reset date, which may be a few weeks or a couple of months after the RBI announcement.

**If your loan is older** (MCLR or base rate), it follows the bank's internal benchmark, which moves more slowly and not always fully.

## What 0.25% means in rupees

Take a ₹50 lakh loan over 20 years at 7.75%. The EMI is **₹41,047**.

| Rate change | New EMI (same tenure) | Or: same EMI, longer tenure | Extra interest if tenure is stretched |
|---|---|---|---|
| +0.25% | ₹41,822 (+₹775) | +12 months | about ₹4.8 lakh |
| +0.5% | ₹42,603 (+₹1,556) | +25 months | about ₹10.3 lakh |
| +1% | ₹44,186 (+₹3,138) | +62 months | about ₹25.3 lakh |
| −0.25% | ₹40,280 (−₹768) | −10 months | saves interest |

*Calculated at the start of the loan; later in the loan the same change moves the EMI less. With five years left, a 0.25% rise adds about ₹240 to this EMI.*

Look at the first row. Paying ₹775 a month more costs about ₹9,300 a year. Keeping the EMI and adding 12 months costs roughly ₹4.8 lakh in total interest, because the extra year comes at the end, at a full EMI of ₹41,047 a month.

## Why your EMI didn't change but your loan got longer

When rates rise, many banks keep the EMI the same and extend the tenure, because it avoids a sudden jump in the instalment. In the 2022–23 rate-rise cycle, this pushed some borrowers' remaining tenure far beyond what they'd signed up for, and in August 2023 the RBI issued rules on how such resets must be handled.

There's a limit. If the rate rises enough, the old EMI barely covers the monthly interest and the loan stops shrinking. In the ₹50 lakh example, a 2.5% rise means the original EMI no longer covers the interest at all, so the bank has to raise it.

**Your options at a reset.** The RBI's rules on [resetting floating rates](https://www.rbi.org.in/commonman/Upload/English/FAQs/PDFs/FAQRFIR10012025.pdf) (updated to October 2025) say that when a benchmark rise resets rates, the lender must offer you:

- a **higher EMI**, a **longer tenure**, or a mix of both;
- a **switch to a fixed rate** for the rest of the loan, where the lender offers one (since October 2025 this is optional for lenders);
- **prepayment**, in part or in full, at any time.

Lenders also have to tell you about any change in EMI or tenure, and send a **quarterly statement** showing principal and interest paid, the EMI, the number of EMIs left and the annual rate. Check it after every RBI decision.

## What to do after an RBI decision

**When rates rise:**

1. Check your next quarterly statement or net banking for the new rate and EMIs left.
2. If the tenure has grown, ask the bank to **raise the EMI instead**, if you can afford it.
3. Consider a part-prepayment; even one extra EMI a year offsets much of a 0.25% rise.

**When rates fall:**

1. Make sure the cut reaches you at the next reset. If you're on MCLR or base rate, ask about moving to the repo-linked rate.
2. Choose whether to **keep the EMI** (shorter tenure, more interest saved) or **lower the EMI** (more monthly cash). The [ET Digital explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mN9p6Tb2cTY) shows that keeping the EMI saves noticeably more interest.

**Either way:** compare your spread with what new borrowers get. If yours is clearly higher, ask your bank to convert, or consider a balance transfer. Since October 2025 the RBI also lets banks reduce the non-credit parts of the spread sooner than the old three-year limit to retain customers, according to [Vinod Kothari's analysis](https://vinodkothari.com/2025/10/rbi-eases-interest-rate-norms-for-lenders/).

## Where rates stand now

The RBI cut the repo rate by a total of 1.25 percentage points during 2025, to 5.25%, and has held it there since. Trade Neeti's [August 2026 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gVSVMF8pouA) walks through the fourth consecutive hold. With retail inflation rising in mid-2026, some analysts expected an increase at the October 2026 meeting. Whatever the decision, the table above tells you roughly what it means for your EMI.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much will my EMI increase if the repo rate goes up by 0.25%?

On a ₹50 lakh, 20-year loan at 7.75%, by about ₹775 a month if the tenure stays the same. Later in the loan the increase is smaller. Many banks keep the EMI and add about a year to the tenure instead, which costs more in total interest.

### When will a repo rate change affect my home loan?

At your loan's next reset date. Repo-linked loans must be reset at least once every three months, so the change usually reaches you within that period. Older MCLR or base-rate loans follow the bank's own benchmark and can lag further.

### Why did my loan tenure increase instead of my EMI?

Banks often keep the EMI unchanged and extend the number of EMIs when rates rise. RBI rules let you choose a higher EMI, a longer tenure or a mix, so you can ask the bank to raise the EMI instead.

### What is the spread on a home loan?

It's the part of your rate above the bank's benchmark, set mainly by your credit risk. It stays fixed for your loan unless your credit assessment changes substantially. A repo change moves the benchmark, not your spread.

### Should I reduce my EMI or tenure when rates fall?

Keeping the EMI and shortening the tenure saves more interest. Lowering the EMI frees monthly cash. If you have no tight budget, keep the EMI.

## The short version

Your rate is repo plus the bank's markup plus your spread, and a repo change reaches you at the next reset. A 0.25% rise adds about ₹775 a month to a ₹50 lakh loan, or about a year of extra EMIs if the bank stretches the tenure. Check your quarterly statement after every RBI decision, and ask for a higher EMI rather than a longer loan.
